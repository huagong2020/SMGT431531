What types of basketball players are there?

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6 · Overview

Player positions (Center, Forward, Guard, or Center, Power Forward, Small Forward, Shooting Guard, Point Guard) in basketball are labels handed down by tradition. A modern roster has centers who shoot threes, forwards who run the offense, and guards who never handle the ball.

Given 14 per-minute statistics such as points per 36 minutes for 360 NBA players, how many kinds of players are there, and which players are alike?

Ask for a definition of “alike” before showing any algorithm. Every answer students give is a distance choice.

Clustering is a modeling decision

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6 · Overview

There is no outcome to check against. The analyst chooses:

features→transformation and weights→distance→algorithm→k. \text{features}\ \rightarrow\ \text{transformation and weights}\ \rightarrow\ \text{distance}\ \rightarrow\ \text{algorithm}\ \rightarrow\ k.

The algorithm computes a consequence of those choices. Validation asks whether the result is real, stable, and useful.

kk: chosen number of clusters for methods requiring one. Density-based clustering methods can determine a cluster count from other tuning choices.

Five decisions in a dissimilarity

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.1 · Unsupervised Learning: Goals, Features, and Distances

Akhanli and Hennig (2023):

  1. Representation: totals confound role with minutes; per-36 rates and shot shares isolate style.
  2. Transformation: a square root transformation.
  3. Standardization: zik=(xik−x‾k)/skz_{ik}=(x_{ik}-\bar x_k)/s_k, or range or robust scaling.
  4. Weighting: dw=∑kwk(zik−zjk)2d_w=\sqrt{\sum_kw_k(z_{ik}-z_{jk})^2} is a weighted euclidean distance; standardization is itself an equal-weight choice.
  5. Aggregation: Minkowski dq=(∑kwk|zik−zjk|q)1/qd_q=\left(\sum_kw_k|z_{ik}-z_{jk}|^q\right)^{1/q} with q = 2 (Euclidean) and q =1 (Manhattan).

dist() returns Euclidean distance on whatever it is given. That is a default, not a decision.

i,ji,j: players; kk: feature index on this slide. xikx_{ik}: original feature; x‾k,sk\bar x_k,s_k: feature mean and positive sample SD; zikz_{ik}: standardized value. wk≥0w_k\ge0: feature weight. dwd_w: weighted Euclidean distance. dqd_q: Minkowski distance with order q≥1q\ge1 (1: Manhattan, 2: Euclidean).

The objective

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.2 · k-Means and k-Medoids

W(C,𝝁)=∑j=1k∑i∈Cj‖𝒙i−𝝁j‖2=∑j=1k12|Cj|∑i,i′∈Cj‖𝒙i−𝒙i′‖2. W(C,\boldsymbol\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i\in C_j}\lVert\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_j\rVert^2 =\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2|C_j|}\sum_{i,i'\in C_j}\lVert\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_{i'}\rVert^2.

At cluster means, the two expressions agree. Common algorithms improve a starting grouping but can stop before finding the best possible grouping. Try several starts and keep the result with the smallest WW.

WW: within-cluster sum of squared distances. i,i′i,i': observation indices; the double sum counts ordered pairs, including zero self-distances.

Lloyd’s algorithm

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.2 · k-Means and k-Medoids
  1. Assign each observation to its nearest center.
  2. Update each center to the mean of its members.
  3. Repeat until nothing changes.

Each step minimizes WW with the other variables fixed, so WW never increases.

k-means++ seeding

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.2 · k-Means and k-Medoids

Choose the first center uniformly, each later center with

P(choose 𝒙i)=D(𝒙i)2∑i′D(𝒙i′)2, P(\text{choose }\mathbf x_i)=\frac{D(\mathbf x_i)^2}{\sum_{i'}D(\mathbf x_{i'})^2},

where DD is the distance to the nearest center chosen so far. Seeds spread out; the expected objective is within O(log⁡k)O(\log k) of optimal before any iteration.

Always run many starts and keep the best. Seeding and nstart are not optional.

𝒙i\mathbf x_i: candidate observation; D(𝒙i)D(\mathbf x_i): Euclidean distance to its nearest existing seed; i′i': index over all candidates; kk: requested cluster count. O(log⁡k)O(\log k) describes an expected approximation factor growing at most as a constant times log⁡k\log k. Multiple starts improve robustness without guaranteeing a global optimum.

Three criteria for kk

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.2 · k-Means and k-Medoids

Elbow: inspect where WkW_k, the fitted within-cluster sum of squares for kk clusters, stops falling quickly.

Silhouette: for observation ii,

si=bi−aimax⁡(ai,bi). s_i=\frac{b_i-a_i}{\max(a_i,b_i)}.

aia_i: mean distance to other members of its cluster; bib_i: smallest mean distance to any other cluster. Values near 1 indicate separation; negative values indicate a closer alternative cluster. Singletons conventionally receive 0. Choose a large average silhouette.

Gap statistic: compares observed within-cluster dispersion with simulated reference datasets. Its dispersion definition and selection rule must match the R call (next slide).

Gap statistic: dispersion and the selection rule

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.2 · k-Means and k-Medoids

For kk clusters with within-cluster dispersion QkQ_k,

Gap⁡(k)=E*[log⁡Qk*]−log⁡Qk,sk=SD⁡*(log⁡Qk*)1+1/B. \operatorname{Gap}(k)=E^*[\log Q_k^*]-\log Q_k,\qquad s_k=\operatorname{SD}_*(\log Q_k^*)\sqrt{1+1/B}.

Qk*Q_k^*: dispersion in a reference dataset; E*E^* and SD⁡*\operatorname{SD}_*: mean and sample SD over BB reference simulations. sks_k is a simulation uncertainty adjustment, not a silhouette.

For Euclidean distances, define QkQ_k as the sum over clusters of all within-cluster pair distances raised to a chosen power, divided by twice the cluster size. Power 2 gives the k-means sum of squares; clusGap() defaults to power 1. The bundled illustration uses that default.

The original adjacent-gap rule selects the smallest kk with Gap⁡(k)≥Gap⁡(k+1)−sk+1\operatorname{Gap}(k)\ge\operatorname{Gap}(k+1)-s_{k+1} (method = "Tibs2001SEmax").

The tutorial instead uses maxSE(..., method = "firstSEmax"), selecting the smallest kk within one uncertainty adjustment of the first local maximum. State which rule produced the selected count.

Primary reference: https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/cluster/html/clusGap.html. The local tutorial and slide-image generator use clusGap’s default d.power = 1. The tutorial chooses the count with firstSEmax; it is distinct from Tibs2001SEmax.

Agglomerative clustering

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.3 · Hierarchical Clustering
  1. every observation is a cluster;
  2. merge the two closest clusters;
  3. update distances to the new cluster;
  4. repeat until one cluster remains.

The only decision: the distance between clusters, given distances between points. That is the linkage.

The EM algorithm

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.4 · Gaussian Mixture Models and the EM Algorithm

How a DBSCAN cluster grows

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.5 · Beyond the Classics: Density, Graphs, and Matrix Factorization

With ε=2.2\varepsilon=2.2 feet and m=5m=5, a shot needs four other nearby shots to be core.

  1. Start at a core point and add its neighbors.
  2. Expand through any new core points, following the dense region.
  3. Stop at border points, which join the cluster but cannot extend it.

Shots reaching no core point remain noise, label 0 in R. No cluster count kk is required.

A point with two other neighbors can be border if it is within epsilon of a core point. Border-only chains cannot bridge dense groups. A border point touching multiple clusters can have an order-dependent assignment. Source: Tutorial 6.5.

Why the Laplacian helps

SGMT 431 · Lecture 6.5 · Beyond the Classics: Density, Graphs, and Matrix Factorization

Give node ii a value fif_i. The Laplacian measures disagreement across links:

𝒇𝖳L𝒇=12∑i,jWij(fi−fj)2. \mathbf f^{\mathsf T}L\mathbf f=\frac12\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2.

Strong links make different values costly. Weak links make differences cheaper.

The normalized Laplacian adjusts for each node’s total link strength:

Lsym=I−D−1/2WD−1/2. L_{\mathrm{sym}}=I-D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}.

Eigenvectors with small eigenvalues supply new coordinates that help separate weakly linked groups.

fif_i: scalar at node ii. WW: symmetric nonnegative similarity matrix. Dii=∑jWijD_{ii}=\sum_jW_{ij}: degree. II: identity. Handle zero degrees separately. Normalized eigenvectors are smooth after adjustment by square-root degree.

For one undirected link with values 0 and 1, weight 1 contributes energy 1, whereas weight 0.1 contributes 0.1. Normalized spectral clustering approximates a normalized-cut objective, which accounts for group connection strength. Source: Tutorial 6.5 and von Luxburg (2007).
