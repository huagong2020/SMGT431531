6.4 Gaussian Mixture Models and the EM Algorithm

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • write the likelihood of a Gaussian mixture model and interpret its parameters;
  • derive the E-step and M-step of the EM algorithm and explain why the log-likelihood never decreases;
  • implement EM for a two-dimensional mixture and interpret the responsibilities as soft cluster memberships;
  • explain the covariance families in mclust and choose among them with BIC;
  • show that k-means is a limiting case of EM; and
  • describe the Bayesian view of mixtures, including Dirichlet priors and the Dirichlet process.

2 Clustering as a probability model

k-means says nothing about how the data were generated. A mixture model does: each observation comes from one of KK components, chosen with probabilities π1,…,πK\pi_1,\dots,\pi_K, and each component is a distribution. With Gaussian components, the density of an observation is

p(𝒙)=∑k=1Kπkϕ(𝒙∣𝝁k,Σk),∑kπk=1, p(\mathbf x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\phi(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k), \qquad \sum_k\pi_k=1,

where ϕ\phi is the multivariate normal density. The parameters are the mixing weights, the component means, and the component covariances. The log-likelihood of nn independent observations is

ℓ(θ)=∑i=1nlog⁡∑k=1Kπkϕ(𝒙i∣𝝁k,Σk). \ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\phi(\mathbf x_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k).

The log of a sum has no closed-form maximizer, which is why a dedicated algorithm is needed.

2.1 Responsibilities

Introduce the unobserved component label zi∈{1,…,K}z_i\in\{1,\dots,K\}. By Bayes’ rule, the posterior probability that observation ii came from component kk is the responsibility

rik=P(zi=k∣𝒙i)=πkϕ(𝒙i∣𝝁k,Σk)∑lπlϕ(𝒙i∣𝝁l,Σl). r_{ik}=P(z_i=k\mid\mathbf x_i)=\frac{\pi_k\,\phi(\mathbf x_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{l}\pi_l\,\phi(\mathbf x_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_l,\Sigma_l)}.

Responsibilities are the soft cluster memberships. A player with ri1=0.55r_{i1}=0.55 and ri2=0.45r_{i2}=0.45 is between two archetypes, and the model says so instead of forcing a choice.

3 The EM algorithm

The expectation-maximization algorithm alternates two steps from an initial parameter guess.

E-step. Compute the responsibilities rikr_{ik} from the current parameters.

M-step. Maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood, which gives closed-form updates:

nk=∑irik,πk=nkn,𝝁k=1nk∑irik𝒙i,Σk=1nk∑irik(𝒙i−𝝁k)(𝒙i−𝝁k)𝖳. n_k=\sum_i r_{ik}, \qquad \pi_k=\frac{n_k}{n}, \qquad \boldsymbol\mu_k=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_i r_{ik}\mathbf x_i, \qquad \Sigma_k=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_i r_{ik}(\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^{\mathsf T}.

Each update is a weighted mean or weighted covariance, with the responsibilities as weights.

3.1 Why it works

For any distribution qq over the labels, Jensen’s inequality gives a lower bound on the log-likelihood,

log⁡∑kπkϕk(𝒙i)=log⁡∑kqik(πkϕk(𝒙i)qik)≥∑kqiklog⁡(πkϕk(𝒙i)qik) \begin{align} \log \sum_k \pi_k \phi_k(\mathbf{x}_i) &= \log \sum_k q_{ik} \left( \frac{\pi_k \phi_k(\mathbf{x}_i)}{q_{ik}} \right) \\ &\ge \sum_k q_{ik} \log \left( \frac{\pi_k \phi_k(\mathbf{x}_i)}{q_{ik}} \right) \end{align}

with equality when qik=rikq_{ik}=r_{ik}. The E-step makes the bound tight at the current parameters; the M-step raises the bound. Together they raise the log-likelihood, so exact EM does not decrease ℓ\ell. Under regularity conditions it approaches a stationary point, which need not be a global maximum. Degenerate covariance matrices are a separate failure mode. As with k-means, the local maximum reached depends on the start.

3.2 The problem: memberships and parameters depend on each other

Suppose four players have the following values of one illustrative performance measure:

x=(1,2,5,6). x=(1,\;2,\;5,\;6).

We fit a mixture of two Gaussian components. Each component has a mean, a variance, and a mixing weight describing its share of the population.

The challenge is circular: estimating each component’s parameters requires knowing which players belong to it, but estimating membership requires knowing the parameters. EM handles this by alternating between soft membership estimates and parameter updates.

3.3 Step 0: Choose initial parameter values

Start with:

Parameter Component 1 Component 2
Mixing weight πk\pi_k 0.50 0.50
Mean μk\mu_k 2 5
Variance σk2\sigma_k^2 4 4

Both initial standard deviations are 4=2\sqrt{4}=2.

Why this step is needed: EM needs a starting model before it can calculate memberships. These values are initial guesses; the following steps update them using the data.

3.4 Step 1: E-step — calculate soft memberships

For each player, ask: under the current model, how much responsibility should each component receive for this observation?

For component 1,

ri1=π1ϕ(xi∣μ1,σ12)π1ϕ(xi∣μ1,σ12)+π2ϕ(xi∣μ2,σ22). r_{i1} = \frac{\pi_1\phi(x_i\mid\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)} {\pi_1\phi(x_i\mid\mu_1,\sigma_1^2) +\pi_2\phi(x_i\mid\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)}.

Here, ϕ\phi is the normal density, evaluated at the observed value. The densities themselves are not membership probabilities. Multiplying by the mixing weights and dividing by the total gives responsibilities that sum to one.

For the player with x=1x=1, the current normal densities are approximately

ϕ(1∣2,4)=0.17603,ϕ(1∣5,4)=0.02700. \phi(1\mid2,4)=0.17603, \qquad \phi(1\mid5,4)=0.02700.

Therefore,

r11=0.5(0.17603)0.5(0.17603)+0.5(0.02700)≈0.867. r_{11} = \frac{0.5(0.17603)} {0.5(0.17603)+0.5(0.02700)} \approx0.867.

Component 2 receives the remaining responsibility: r12=1−0.867=0.133r_{12}=1-0.867=0.133.

Repeating this calculation gives:

Observed value xix_i Responsibility for component 1 Responsibility for component 2
1 0.867 0.133
2 0.755 0.245
5 0.245 0.755
6 0.133 0.867

Purpose of the E-step: estimate the missing memberships using the current parameters. Every player can contribute to both components, with a larger contribution to the component that better explains their value.

The parameters stay fixed during this step.

3.5 Step 2: M-step — update the parameters

Now hold the responsibilities fixed and use them as weights to update the model.

A. Update the mixing weights: how much of the sample does each component represent?

Add the responsibilities to obtain an effective count:

n1=0.867+0.755+0.245+0.133=2,n2=2. n_1=0.867+0.755+0.245+0.133=2, \qquad n_2=2.

These are fractional membership totals, rather than counts of players assigned exclusively to a component. The updated mixing weights are

π1new=24=0.5,π2new=24=0.5. \pi_1^{\text{new}}=\frac{2}{4}=0.5, \qquad \pi_2^{\text{new}}=\frac{2}{4}=0.5.

They remain equal because this example is symmetric. In other datasets, the weights can change.

B. Update the means: where should each component be centered?

Calculate a weighted average:

μ1new=0.867(1)+0.755(2)+0.245(5)+0.133(6)2≈2.200. \mu_1^{\text{new}} = \frac{0.867(1)+0.755(2)+0.245(5)+0.133(6)}{2} \approx2.200.

Similarly, μ2new≈4.800\mu_2^{\text{new}}\approx4.800. The smaller observations have more influence on component 1’s mean, while the larger observations have more influence on component 2’s mean.

C. Update the variances: how spread out should each component be?

Calculate weighted squared distances from the new mean:

(σ12)new=0.867(1−2.200)2+0.755(2−2.200)22+0.245(5−2.200)2+0.133(6−2.200)22≈2.560. \begin{aligned} (\sigma_1^2)^{\text{new}} &=\frac{0.867(1-2.200)^2+0.755(2-2.200)^2}{2}\\ &\quad+\frac{0.245(5-2.200)^2+0.133(6-2.200)^2}{2}\\ &\approx2.560. \end{aligned}

Component 2 also has updated variance 2.5602.560, again because of symmetry. Both standard deviations decrease from 22 to approximately 1.6001.600.

Purpose of the M-step: find the parameters that best fit the current fractional memberships. The weights describe component size, the means describe location, and the variances describe spread.

All M-step updates use the same responsibilities calculated in the preceding E-step. Calculations here use unrounded values; displayed values are rounded.

3.6 Why do we need Jensen’s inequality?

We want to maximize the observed-data log-likelihood:

ℓ(θ)=∑ilog⁡[∑kπkϕ(xi∣μk,σk2)]. \ell(\theta) = \sum_i\log\left[ \sum_k\pi_k\phi(x_i\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2) \right].

The difficulty is the sum inside the logarithm. Each observation could come from either component, so their parameters appear together inside the same expression. This prevents us from estimating each component separately using ordinary mean and variance formulas.

Jensen’s inequality gives us an easier expression to optimize. For fixed membership weights rik>0r_{ik}>0 that sum to one for each observation,

ℓ(θ)=∑ilog⁡[∑krikπkϕ(xi∣μk,σk2)rik]≥∑i,kriklog⁡[πkϕ(xi∣μk,σk2)rik]⏟B(θ;r): a lower bound. \begin{aligned} \ell(\theta) &= \sum_i\log\left[ \sum_k r_{ik} \frac{\pi_k\phi(x_i\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)}{r_{ik}} \right]\\ &\ge \underbrace{ \sum_{i,k}r_{ik} \log\left[ \frac{\pi_k\phi(x_i\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)}{r_{ik}} \right] }_{B(\theta;r):\text{ a lower bound}}. \end{aligned}

This follows because the logarithm is concave: the log of a weighted average is at least the weighted average of the logs. Here θ\theta denotes all mixture parameters; rr denotes the responsibilities held fixed while considering changes to those parameters.

The useful change is that the lower bound contains a sum of separate log terms, making the parameter updates manageable.

EM uses this bound in two coordinated steps:

  1. E-step: calculate responsibilities using the current parameters. This choice makes the lower bound equal the actual log-likelihood at the current model.
  2. M-step: hold those responsibilities fixed and choose parameters that maximize the lower bound.

Consequently,

ℓ(θnew)≥B(θnew;r)≥B(θold;r)=ℓ(θold). \begin{aligned} \ell(\theta_{\text{new}}) &\ge B(\theta_{\text{new}};r)\\ &\ge B(\theta_{\text{old}};r) =\ell(\theta_{\text{old}}). \end{aligned}

Read this from the last expression backward: we start with a bound that touches the current likelihood, raise that bound, and the new likelihood must be at least as high.

Purpose: Jensen’s inequality explains why these simpler alternating updates improve—or leave unchanged—the objective we actually care about. It is part of the justification; we do not calculate the inequality separately during every iteration.

3.7 Why are the M-step mean and variance weighted averages?

During the M-step, the responsibilities are fixed. Maximizing the lower bound is therefore equivalent to maximizing

Q(θ)=∑i,krik[logπk+logϕ(xi∣μk,σk2)], Q(\theta) = \sum_{i,k}r_{ik} \left[ \log\pi_k+ \log\phi(x_i\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2) \right],

because the remaining term, −∑i,kriklog⁡rik-\sum_{i,k}r_{ik}\log r_{ik}, does not depend on the parameters being updated.

For one Gaussian component, the part involving its mean and variance is

Qk=−nk2log⁡(σk2)−12σk2∑irik(xi−μk)2+constant,nk=∑irik. Q_k = -\frac{n_k}{2}\log(\sigma_k^2) -\frac{1}{2\sigma_k^2} \sum_i r_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)^2 +\text{constant}, \qquad n_k=\sum_i r_{ik}.

This expression tells us where both update formulas come from.

Why update the mean this way? With variance held fixed, maximizing QkQ_k means minimizing the weighted squared distances:

∑irik(xi−μk)2. \sum_i r_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)^2.

An observation with high responsibility should pull the component’s center more strongly than an observation with low responsibility. The minimizing value is the weighted mean:

μknew=∑irikxi∑irik. \boxed{ \mu_k^{\text{new}} = \frac{\sum_i r_{ik}x_i}{\sum_i r_{ik}} }.

For readers familiar with differentiation, the minimum satisfies

−2∑irik(xi−μk)=0, -2\sum_i r_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)=0,

which rearranges directly to that formula.

In our example, x=1x=1 receives weight 0.8670.867 in component 1, while x=6x=6 receives only 0.1330.133. The smaller observation therefore has much more influence on this component’s center.

Purpose: locate the center that best fits the current fractional memberships.

Why update the variance this way? After updating the mean, define the weighted squared spread around it:

Sk=∑irik(xi−μknew)2. S_k=\sum_i r_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k^{\text{new}})^2.

Writing v=σk2v=\sigma_k^2, we maximize

Qk(v)=−nk2log⁡v−Sk2v+constant. Q_k(v)=-\frac{n_k}{2}\log v-\frac{S_k}{2v} +\text{constant}.

These terms balance two effects:

  • A variance that is too large spreads the density too thinly, through the −nk2log⁡v-\frac{n_k}{2}\log v term.
  • A variance that is too small gives observations away from the mean very low density, through the −Sk2v-\frac{S_k}{2v} term.

Setting the derivative to zero gives

−nk2v+Sk2v2=0, -\frac{n_k}{2v}+\frac{S_k}{2v^2}=0,

so

(σk2)new=Sknk=∑irik(xi−μknew)2∑irik. \boxed{ (\sigma_k^2)^{\text{new}} = \frac{S_k}{n_k} = \frac{\sum_i r_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k^{\text{new}})^2} {\sum_i r_{ik}} }.

Purpose: choose the spread that maximizes the Gaussian likelihood under the current fractional memberships.

We use the new mean because the variance describes spread around the updated center. We divide by nkn_k because this is a maximum-likelihood estimate; the familiar n−1n-1 correction serves a different purpose, producing an unbiased sample variance for a known group.

These updates assume nk>0n_k>0 and a positive fitted variance. If the weighted squared spread is zero, the unconstrained component collapses; use the covariance safeguards discussed elsewhere in this tutorial.

If memberships were known and every responsibility were either zero or one, these formulas would become the usual component mean and maximum-likelihood variance. EM extends those calculations to uncertain memberships by using fractional weights. With multiple features, the variance update becomes the weighted covariance matrix shown above.

3.8 Step 3: Repeat — the updated model changes the memberships

One E-step followed by one M-step completes an EM iteration. After the first iteration:

Parameter Component 1 Component 2
Mixing weight 0.500 0.500
Mean 2.200 4.800
Variance 2.560 2.560

We must calculate responsibilities again because the means and variances have changed. For example, the responsibility of component 1 for x=1x=1 increases from approximately 0.8670.867 to 0.9270.927.

Further iterations produce:

Completed iterations Mean 1 Mean 2 Variance of each component Observed-data log-likelihood
0: initial guesses 2.000 5.000 4.000 −8.623

The observed-data log-likelihood measures how well the full mixture explains the observations. Larger values, including less negative values, indicate a better fit to these same data.

Stop when its improvement becomes sufficiently small, subject to a maximum iteration limit. Exact EM does not decrease this likelihood, but different initial guesses can lead to different solutions.

What to remember: the E-step uses the current model to estimate membership weights. The M-step uses those weights to update the model. Jensen’s inequality connects these easier steps to improvement in the original likelihood.

5 k-means as a limiting case

Fix every covariance to σ2I\sigma^2 I with the same σ\sigma and equal weights πk=1/K\pi_k=1/K. The responsibilities become

rik∝exp⁡(−‖𝒙i−𝝁k‖22σ2), r_{ik}\propto\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),

and as σ→0\sigma\to0 each rikr_{ik} goes to 1 for the nearest center and 0 elsewhere. The E-step becomes the k-means assignment step and the M-step becomes the k-means update. Lloyd k-means corresponds to the hard-assignment limit with equal mixing weights and a common spherical variance. Finite-variance EM uses soft responsibilities, so the algorithms are not identical. Everything k-means assumes, spherical clusters of equal spread with hard membership, is a restriction the mixture model can relax.

6 Covariance families and model selection

A full covariance in pp dimensions has p(p+1)/2p(p+1)/2 parameters per component, which is 105105 for the 14-feature profiles. With 360 players and several components that is too many. mclust parameterizes each covariance through its eigendecomposition,

Σk=λkDkAkDk𝖳, \Sigma_k=\lambda_k D_k A_k D_k^{\mathsf T},

where λk\lambda_k is the volume, AkA_k the shape (a diagonal matrix of normalized eigenvalues), and DkD_k the orientation. Each of the three can be equal across components (E), variable (V), or, for shape and orientation, the identity (I). Model EII is spherical with equal volume (the k-means geometry); VVV is fully unrestricted; VEE shares shape and orientation but lets volumes differ, and so on.

6.1 What does a covariance matrix describe?

Suppose we measure two features for players: xx and yy. A component’s covariance matrix is

Σ=(Var⁡(x)Cov⁡(x,y)Cov⁡(x,y)Var⁡(y)). \Sigma= \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{Var}(x)&\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)\\ \operatorname{Cov}(x,y)&\operatorname{Var}(y) \end{pmatrix}.

  • The diagonal entries describe how much each feature varies.
  • The off-diagonal entries describe whether the features tend to move together.

In two dimensions, a Gaussian component has elliptical density contours. Its covariance controls the ellipse’s size, elongation, and direction. Its mean controls the ellipse’s location.

6.2 A numerical covariance example

Consider four hypothetical players measured on two illustrative feature scales:

Player xx yy
A 3 3
B 4 6
C 6 4
D 7 7

To focus on the calculation, assume these four players belong completely to one component.

Step A: Find the center.

x‾=3+4+6+74=5,y‾=3+6+4+74=5. \bar{x}=\frac{3+4+6+7}{4}=5, \qquad \bar{y}=\frac{3+6+4+7}{4}=5.

Subtract the center from each observation:

Player xi−5x_i-5 yi−5y_i-5 Product of deviations
A −2 −2 4
B −1 1 −1
C 1 −1 −1
D 2 2 4

Why subtract the means? We want to describe variation around the component’s center, independently of where that center is located.

Step B: Calculate the variances.

Using the maximum-likelihood convention from the EM updates,

Var⁡(x)=(−2)2+(−1)2+12+224=2.5. \operatorname{Var}(x) = \frac{(-2)^2+(-1)^2+1^2+2^2}{4} =2.5.

Similarly, Var⁡(y)=2.5\operatorname{Var}(y)=2.5.

We divide by 44, rather than 4−14-1, to match the Gaussian maximum-likelihood calculation. The usual unbiased sample covariance uses n−1n-1.

Step C: Calculate the covariance.

Cov⁡(x,y)=(−2)(−2)+(−1)(1)+(1)(−1)+(2)(2)4=1.5. \operatorname{Cov}(x,y) = \frac{(-2)(-2)+(-1)(1)+(1)(-1)+(2)(2)}{4} =1.5.

Same-sign deviations contribute positively; opposite-sign deviations contribute negatively. Here the positive contributions dominate.

The resulting covariance matrix is

Σ=(2.51.51.52.5). \boxed{ \Sigma= \begin{pmatrix} 2.5&1.5\\ 1.5&2.5 \end{pmatrix} }.

Both features have standard deviation 2.5≈1.581\sqrt{2.5}\approx1.581. Their correlation is

ρ=1.52.52.5=0.6. \rho = \frac{1.5}{\sqrt{2.5}\sqrt{2.5}} =0.6.

Interpretation: players above the component mean on xx tend also to be above it on yy. The Gaussian ellipse tilts upward.

With uncertain component memberships, EM uses the same calculation with responsibilities as weights:

Σk=∑irik(𝒙i−𝝁k)(𝒙i−𝝁k)𝖳∑irik. \Sigma_k = \frac{\sum_i r_{ik} (\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k) (\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^{\mathsf T}} {\sum_i r_{ik}}.

Each player influences the covariance in proportion to their responsibility for that component.

6.3 How covariance becomes volume, shape, and orientation

The lecture writes

Σk=λkDkAkDk𝖳. \Sigma_k=\lambda_kD_kA_kD_k^{\mathsf T}.

These pieces describe different aspects of the ellipse:

Piece Meaning Question it answers
λk\lambda_k Overall scale, controlling volume How large is the component?
AkA_k Relative spread along its principal axes How elongated is it?
DkD_k Directions of those axes Which way does it point?

For our example,

Σ=2⏟λ12(1−111)⏟D(2000.5)⏟AD𝖳. \Sigma = \underbrace{2}_{\lambda} \underbrace{ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1&-1\\ 1&1 \end{pmatrix} }_{D} \underbrace{ \begin{pmatrix} 2&0\\ 0&0.5 \end{pmatrix} }_{A} D^{\mathsf T}.

The principal-axis variances are 44 and 11, so the corresponding standard deviations are 22 and 11.

  • Volume: λ=det⁡(Σ)=2\lambda=\sqrt{\det(\Sigma)}=2. At a fixed density-contour level relative to the peak, ellipse area is proportional to this scale.
  • Shape: the ellipse’s long axis is twice as long as its short axis.
  • Orientation: the long axis points along y=xy=x, at 45∘45^\circ to the horizontal.

The diagonal entries of AA multiply to one. This normalization separates overall scale from relative axis lengths.

6.4 Reading covariance-family names

In mclust, the three letters describe volume, shape, and orientation, in that order:

  • E: equal across components.
  • V: allowed to vary across components.
  • I: identity restriction. For shape, all axis variances are equal; for orientation, axes align with the original feature coordinates.

“Equal” describes a constraint between components. It does not mean that the components have equal means or mixing weights.

6.5 A concrete comparison: EEE versus VEE

Suppose component 1 has our example covariance:

Σ1=(2.51.51.52.5). \Sigma_1= \begin{pmatrix} 2.5&1.5\\ 1.5&2.5 \end{pmatrix}.

Under EEE, component 2 must have exactly the same covariance:

Σ2=Σ1. \Sigma_2=\Sigma_1.

Their ellipses have the same size, elongation, and direction, although their centers can differ.

Under VEE, component 2 may instead have

Σ2=2Σ1=(5335). \Sigma_2=2\Sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 5&3\\ 3&5 \end{pmatrix}.

This ellipse has the same elongation and direction, but larger volume. At the same contour level relative to each component’s peak, its axis lengths increase by 2\sqrt{2}, because lengths depend on standard deviations rather than variances.

Why restrict covariance families? Sharing aspects of covariance reduces the number of parameters to estimate. More flexible families can fit more complicated patterns, but need more information. BIC balances improved likelihood against the additional parameters.

The Bayesian information criterion trades fit against parameters,

BIC=2ℓ(θ̂)−mlog⁡n, \mathrm{BIC}=2\ell(\hat\theta)-m\log n,

where mm is the number of parameters (mclust reports it in this sign convention, so larger is better). BIC penalizes both extra components and richer covariance structures, and for mixtures it is the standard selection tool.

7 The Bayesian view

The mixture model is a hierarchical model, and Lectures 4 and 5 supply the language. Write it as

zi∼Categorical(𝝅),𝒙i∣zi=k∼N(𝝁k,Σk), z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\boldsymbol\pi), \qquad \mathbf x_i\mid z_i=k\sim N(\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k),

with priors on the parameters:

𝝅∼Dirichlet(α1,…,αK),𝝁k∼N(𝒎0,S0),Σk∼Inverse-Wishart(ν0,Ψ0). \boldsymbol\pi\sim\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_K), \qquad \boldsymbol\mu_k\sim N(\mathbf m_0,S_0), \qquad \Sigma_k\sim\text{Inverse-Wishart}(\nu_0,\Psi_0).

The Dirichlet is the conjugate prior for the mixing weights, exactly as the beta was for one proportion in Lecture 4. Three things follow.

  • EM estimates parameters. Ordinary mixture EM maximizes the observed-data likelihood. Responsibilities condition on those fitted parameters and have a plug-in latent-variable interpretation. Full Bayes integrates over parameter uncertainty as well. Gibbs updates use full conditional distributions derived from both likelihood and priors, rather than simply randomizing the M-step.
  • Priors regularize. A suitable proper covariance prior or an explicit eigenvalue floor can discourage or exclude component collapse. A good initialization alone cannot guarantee protection against a singular solution. The prior must control behavior near zero variance.
  • The number of components can be learned. With a symmetric Dirichlet prior whose concentration α/K\alpha/K shrinks as KK grows, the model in the limit K→∞K\to\infty is a Dirichlet process mixture. For a sample of size nn, it allows 1 through nn occupied components and puts a posterior distribution on that count, which replaces the BIC search with posterior inference. The price is heavier computation and a result that depends on the concentration parameter.
NoteLabel switching

The mixture likelihood is unchanged by relabeling the components, so any sampler that explores the posterior fully will visit every permutation of the labels. Posterior means of 𝝁k\boldsymbol\mu_k are then meaningless unless the labels are aligned across draws. It is the mixture-model version of the identification problem in Lecture 5, and the usual remedies are an ordering constraint or post-hoc relabeling.

9 Key takeaways

  • A Gaussian mixture is a generative model whose responsibilities are soft memberships.
  • EM alternates computing responsibilities with weighted parameter updates and never decreases the log-likelihood.
  • Lloyd k-means is a hard-assignment, small-variance limit under equal mixing weights and spherical covariance.
  • Covariance families and BIC control complexity; the selected number of components describes the density, not necessarily the analyst’s archetypes.
  • The Bayesian mixture adds Dirichlet and inverse-Wishart priors, Gibbs sampling, and the Dirichlet process for an unbounded number of components.
