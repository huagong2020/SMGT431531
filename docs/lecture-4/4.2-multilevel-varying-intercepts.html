4.2 Multilevel Models with Varying Intercepts

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • write a two-level model in level-one and level-two form and in the Laird-Ware matrix form;
  • distinguish fixed effects from random effects and say what “random” means for a set of players;
  • interpret variance components and the intraclass correlation coefficient;
  • derive the shrinkage estimate of a group effect and verify it against lme4;
  • explain the difference between maximum likelihood and restricted maximum likelihood; and
  • fit varying-intercept models for a continuous outcome and a binary outcome.

2 Three ways to estimate a passer’s EPA

Tutorial 4.1 showed that the observed EPA per attempt is a noisy estimate of ability. There are three natural estimators.

Complete pooling ignores the passer entirely and uses the league mean for everyone:

θ̂jpool=y‾. \hat\theta_j^{\text{pool}}=\bar y.

No pooling uses each passer’s own mean:

θ̂jnone=y‾j. \hat\theta_j^{\text{none}}=\bar y_j.

Partial pooling uses a compromise, and the multilevel model tells us the weight.

no_pooling <- passes %>%
  group_by(passer) %>%
  summarize(attempts = n(), epa_mean = mean(qb_epa), .groups = "drop")

complete_pooling <- mean(passes$qb_epa)
complete_pooling
[1] 0.1741105

3 The two-level model

At level one, each attempt is the passer’s mean plus noise:

yij=β0j+εij,εij∼N(0,σ2). y_{ij}=\beta_{0j}+\varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad \varepsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2).

At level two, each passer’s mean is the league mean plus a passer deviation:

β0j=β0+uj,uj∼N(0,τ2). \beta_{0j}=\beta_0+u_j, \qquad u_j\sim N(0,\tau^2).

Substituting gives the composite model

yij=β0+uj+εij. y_{ij}=\beta_0+u_j+\varepsilon_{ij}.

Here β0\beta_0 is a fixed effect: one unknown constant for the whole league. The uju_j are random effects: they are treated as draws from a distribution whose variance τ2\tau^2 is estimated. The word “random” does not mean the passers were sampled at random. It means we model the population of passer effects with a distribution and borrow strength across them.

3.1 Laird-Ware form

For every observation stacked into a vector 𝒚\mathbf y of length NN,

𝒚=X𝜷+Z𝒖+𝜺,𝒖∼N(𝟎,G),𝜺∼N(𝟎,R), \mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\beta+Z\mathbf u+\boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \mathbf u\sim N(\mathbf 0,G), \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim N(\mathbf 0,R),

where XX is the N×pN\times p fixed-effect design matrix, ZZ is the N×JN\times J random-effect design matrix with one indicator column per passer, G=τ2IJG=\tau^2 I_J, and R=σ2INR=\sigma^2 I_N. Marginally,

𝒚∼N(X𝜷,ZGZ𝖳+R). \mathbf y\sim N\!\left(X\boldsymbol\beta,\ ZGZ^{\mathsf T}+R\right).

Two attempts by the same passer share the passer’s uju_j, so their covariance is τ2\tau^2; attempts by different passers are independent. This marginal covariance is what maximum likelihood fits.

4 Fit the model with lme4

The formula (1 | passer) requests a varying intercept for each passer.

epa_fit <- lmer(qb_epa ~ 1 + (1 | passer), data = passes)
summary(epa_fit)$coefficients %>% knitr::kable(digits = 4)
Estimate Std. Error t value
(Intercept) 0.1456 0.0197 7.3992 
variance_components <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(epa_fit))
variance_components %>%
  select(grp, vcov, sdcor) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 4)
grp vcov sdcor
passer 0.0133 0.1151
Residual 2.3716 1.5400

The residual standard deviation is large because a single pass attempt can swing EPA by several points. The passer standard deviation is small in comparison, which is exactly why individual attempts tell us so little.

4.1 The intraclass correlation coefficient

The share of variance that lies between passers is

ρ=τ2τ2+σ2. \rho=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}.

It is also the correlation between two attempts by the same passer.

tau2 <- variance_components$vcov[variance_components$grp == "passer"]
sigma2 <- variance_components$vcov[variance_components$grp == "Residual"]
icc <- tau2 / (tau2 + sigma2)
icc
[1] 0.005556992

Less than one percent of the variance in EPA per attempt is between passers. That does not mean passers do not matter. It means that hundreds of attempts are needed before the between-passer signal emerges from the within-passer noise, which is consistent with the reliability calculation in Tutorial 4.1.

5 Shrinkage: the estimate of each passer’s effect

Conditional on the variance components, the best estimate of uju_j is a weighted compromise between the passer’s own mean and the fixed intercept:

ûj=njτ2njτ2+σ2(y‾j−β̂0)=wj(y‾j−β̂0). \hat u_j=\frac{n_j\tau^2}{n_j\tau^2+\sigma^2}\left(\bar y_j-\hat\beta_0\right) =w_j\left(\bar y_j-\hat\beta_0\right).

The weight wjw_j is the reliability from Tutorial 4.1. A passer with many attempts keeps most of their observed deviation; a passer with few attempts is pulled almost all the way to the league mean.

5.1 Derivation

Treat uju_j as unknown with distribution N(0,τ2)N(0,\tau^2) and the passer’s mean as data with y‾j∣uj∼N(β0+uj,σ2/nj)\bar y_j\mid u_j\sim N(\beta_0+u_j,\ \sigma^2/n_j). Bayes’ rule with a normal prior and normal likelihood gives a normal posterior whose mean is precision-weighted:

E[uj∣y‾j]=njσ2(y‾j−β0)+1τ2⋅0njσ2+1τ2=njτ2njτ2+σ2(y‾j−β0). E[u_j\mid \bar y_j] =\frac{\dfrac{n_j}{\sigma^2}\left(\bar y_j-\beta_0\right)+\dfrac{1}{\tau^2}\cdot 0}{\dfrac{n_j}{\sigma^2}+\dfrac{1}{\tau^2}} =\frac{n_j\tau^2}{n_j\tau^2+\sigma^2}\left(\bar y_j-\beta_0\right).

The estimator is called the best linear unbiased predictor (BLUP) in the mixed-model literature and the posterior mean in the Bayesian literature. Tutorial 4.4 returns to this identity.

5.2 Verify against lme4

beta0 <- fixef(epa_fit)[["(Intercept)"]]

manual <- no_pooling %>%
  mutate(
    weight = shrinkage_weight(attempts, tau2, sigma2),
    u_manual = weight * (epa_mean - beta0)
  )

lme4_effects <- ranef(epa_fit)$passer %>%
  tibble::rownames_to_column("passer") %>%
  rename(u_lme4 = `(Intercept)`)

comparison <- manual %>%
  inner_join(lme4_effects, by = "passer") %>%
  mutate(partial_pooling = beta0 + u_lme4)

6 Estimation: ML and REML

The variance components are estimated by maximizing the marginal likelihood of 𝒚\mathbf y after integrating out 𝒖\mathbf u. There are two versions.

  • Maximum likelihood (ML) maximizes the full marginal likelihood. Variance estimates are biased downward because the fixed effects are estimated from the same data.
  • Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) maximizes the likelihood of linear contrasts of 𝒚\mathbf y that do not depend on 𝜷\boldsymbol\beta. This removes the bias, in the same way that dividing by n−1n-1 instead of nn corrects the sample variance.

lmer() uses REML by default. Use ML (REML = FALSE) when comparing models with different fixed effects by likelihood ratio, because REML likelihoods are not comparable across fixed-effect specifications.

epa_ml <- lmer(qb_epa ~ 1 + (1 | passer), data = passes, REML = FALSE)

data.frame(
  method = c("REML", "ML"),
  tau = c(
    as.data.frame(VarCorr(epa_fit))$sdcor[1],
    as.data.frame(VarCorr(epa_ml))$sdcor[1]
  ),
  sigma = c(sigma(epa_fit), sigma(epa_ml))
) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 4)
method tau sigma
REML 0.1151 1.54
ML 0.1131 1.54

With 17,000 attempts and one fixed effect, the two agree closely. They diverge in small samples.

NoteEmpirical Bayes

The random-effect estimates are posterior means under the prior uj∼N(0,τ̂2)u_j\sim N(0,\hat\tau^2), where τ̂2\hat\tau^2 was estimated from the same data. Estimating the prior from the data and then conditioning on it is called empirical Bayes. Tutorial 4.4 contrasts it with a fully Bayesian analysis that puts a prior on τ2\tau^2 as well.

7 A binary outcome: completion

For completion, the level-one model is a logistic regression with a passer intercept:

logit⁡P(yij=1)=β0+uj,uj∼N(0,τ2). \operatorname{logit}P(y_{ij}=1)=\beta_0+u_j, \qquad u_j\sim N(0,\tau^2).

This is a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). The random effect enters on the log-odds scale, so there is no residual variance parameter. glmer() approximates the marginal likelihood with the Laplace method by default.

completion_fit <- glmer(
  complete ~ 1 + (1 | passer),
  data = passes,
  family = binomial()
)

data.frame(
  intercept = fixef(completion_fit)[["(Intercept)"]],
  league_completion_rate = plogis(fixef(completion_fit)[["(Intercept)"]]),
  tau = as.data.frame(VarCorr(completion_fit))$sdcor[1]
) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
intercept league_completion_rate tau
0.715 0.671 0.102

On the latent logistic scale the residual variance is π2/3≈3.29\pi^2/3\approx 3.29, so the latent intraclass correlation is τ2/(τ2+π2/3)\tau^2/(\tau^2+\pi^2/3).

tau2_logit <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(completion_fit))$vcov[1]
tau2_logit / (tau2_logit + pi^2 / 3)
[1] 0.00312321

7.1 Shrunken completion rates

Converting each passer’s estimate back to a probability gives a shrunken completion rate.

completion_effects <- as.data.frame(ranef(completion_fit, condVar = TRUE)) %>%
  transmute(
    passer = as.character(grp),
    u = condval,
    conditional_sd = condsd
  ) %>%
  inner_join(
    passes %>% group_by(passer) %>%
      summarize(attempts = n(), raw_rate = mean(complete), .groups = "drop") %>%
      mutate(passer = as.character(passer)),
    by = "passer"
  ) %>%
  mutate(shrunken_rate = plogis(fixef(completion_fit)[["(Intercept)"]] + u))

completion_effects %>%
  arrange(desc(raw_rate)) %>%
  select(passer, attempts, raw_rate, shrunken_rate) %>%
  slice(c(1:5, 50:53)) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)

7.2 Uncertainty about each effect

condVar = TRUE returns the conditional variance of each uju_j given the data and the variance components. Under the normal model the conditional variance is

Var⁡(uj∣𝒚)=(1τ2+njσ2)−1=σ2τ2njτ2+σ2, \operatorname{Var}(u_j\mid\mathbf y)=\left(\frac{1}{\tau^2}+\frac{n_j}{\sigma^2}\right)^{-1} =\frac{\sigma^2\tau^2}{n_j\tau^2+\sigma^2},

which shrinks with attempts. The GLMM version uses the Laplace approximation to the same quantity.

completion_effects %>%
  filter(attempts >= 100) %>%
  mutate(passer = reorder(passer, u)) %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = u, y = passer)) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", color = "gray50") +
  geom_errorbarh(
    aes(xmin = u - 1.96 * conditional_sd, xmax = u + 1.96 * conditional_sd),
    height = 0,
    color = "gray60"
  ) +
  geom_point(color = "#03a9e6", size = 1.8) +
  labs(x = "Passer effect on the log-odds of completion", y = NULL) +
  theme_minimal() +
  theme(axis.text.y = element_text(size = 6.5))

Nearly every interval crosses zero. On completion alone, without adjusting for throw difficulty, one season does not separate most passers from the league. Tutorial 4.3 adds the covariates that make the comparison fair.

8 Practice

  1. Write the composite model for completion in level-one and level-two form.
  2. A passer has 60 attempts. With τ2\tau^2 and σ2\sigma^2 from the EPA model, what fraction of their observed deviation is retained?
  3. Why is the intraclass correlation for EPA so small even though quarterback quality clearly matters?
  4. When would you refit with REML = FALSE?

9 Key takeaways

  • A multilevel model estimates the population variance of player effects and uses it to weight each player’s data against the league.
  • The random-effect estimate is the observed deviation multiplied by the reliability njτ2/(njτ2+σ2)n_j\tau^2/(n_j\tau^2+\sigma^2).
  • The intraclass correlation measures how much of per-play variance is between players.
  • REML corrects the small-sample bias of variance estimates; ML is needed for comparing fixed-effect specifications.
  • The same structure extends to binary outcomes through a GLMM.
