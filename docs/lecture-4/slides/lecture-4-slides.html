Completion rates: exact sampling and approximation

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.1 · Player Evaluation and Small Samples

For njn_j conditionally independent attempts with completion probability θj\theta_j:

Sj∣θj∼Binomial⁡(nj,θj),Y‾j=Sj/nj=θj+εj. S_j\mid\theta_j\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n_j,\theta_j),\qquad \bar Y_j=S_j/n_j=\theta_j+\varepsilon_j.

SjS_j: completion count. Y‾j\bar Y_j: random sample rate. εj=Y‾j−θj\varepsilon_j=\bar Y_j-\theta_j: sampling error. Lowercase y‾j\bar y_j denotes the observed rate.

E(Y‾j∣θj)=E(Sj∣θj)/nj=θj,E(εj∣θj)=0,Var⁡(εj∣θj)=θj(1−θj)/nj. \begin{aligned} E(\bar Y_j\mid\theta_j)&=E(S_j\mid\theta_j)/n_j=\theta_j,\\ E(\varepsilon_j\mid\theta_j)&=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_j\mid\theta_j)=\theta_j(1-\theta_j)/n_j. \end{aligned}

These statements are exact. Approximating this discrete error by a normal distribution is a separate step. Replacing θj(1−θj)\theta_j(1-\theta_j) by common σ2\sigma^2 is another simplification.

The additive identity holds for completion rates without assuming normal errors. The normal approximation is most useful when expected makes and misses are both sufficiently large.

Signal plus noise

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.1 · Player Evaluation and Small Samples

Y‾j=θj+εj,Var⁡(Y‾j)=τ2⏟signal+σ2/nj⏟noise. \bar Y_j=\theta_j+\varepsilon_j, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_j)=\underbrace{\tau^2}_{\text{signal}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2/n_j}_{\text{noise}}.

For conditionally independent attempts with common variance σ2\sigma^2, the average error has variance σ2/nj\sigma^2/n_j. The reliability is the signal share,

Rj=τ2τ2+σ2/nj=njnj+σ2/τ2. R_j=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2/n_j}=\frac{n_j}{n_j+\sigma^2/\tau^2}.

σ2/τ2\sigma^2/\tau^2 is the number of attempts at which an average becomes half signal.

jj: player; njn_j: fixed attempt count; Y‾j\bar Y_j: random sample mean; θj\theta_j: underlying mean; εj\varepsilon_j: sample-mean error. Assume E(εj∣θj)=0E(\varepsilon_j\mid\theta_j)=0, Var⁡(θj)=τ2\operatorname{Var}(\theta_j)=\tau^2, and Var⁡(εj)=σ2/nj\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_j)=\sigma^2/n_j. Rj=wjR_j=w_j: reliability. Zero conditional error mean implies Cov⁡(θj,εj)=0\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\varepsilon_j)=0, giving the variance sum. This calculation needs finite variances, not normality. Assume τ2>0\tau^2>0.

Split halves: why correlation measures reliability

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.1 · Player Evaluation and Small Samples

For each passer, put attempts 1, 3, 5, … in the odd half and attempts 2, 4, 6, … in the even half. Correlate the two half-sample averages across passers.

For equal half sizes of mm attempts and stable player means,

Y‾j,o=θj+εj,o,Y‾j,e=θj+εj,e. \bar Y_{j,o}=\theta_j+\varepsilon_{j,o},\qquad \bar Y_{j,e}=\theta_j+\varepsilon_{j,e}.

Cov⁡(Y‾j,o,Y‾j,e)=τ2,Var⁡(Y‾j,o)=Var⁡(Y‾j,e)=τ2+σ2/m,Corr⁡(Y‾j,o,Y‾j,e)=τ2τ2+σ2/m=Rhalf. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}(\bar Y_{j,o},\bar Y_{j,e})&=\tau^2,\\ \operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_{j,o})=\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_{j,e})&=\tau^2+\sigma^2/m,\\ \operatorname{Corr}(\bar Y_{j,o},\bar Y_{j,e})&=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2/m}=R_{\mathrm{half}}. \end{aligned}

jj: passer; o,eo,e: odd and even halves; θj\theta_j: player mean with variance τ2\tau^2; εj,o,εj,e\varepsilon_{j,o},\varepsilon_{j,e}: errors with zero conditional means and variance σ2/m\sigma^2/m. Assume the half errors are conditionally independent given θj\theta_j. σ2\sigma^2: per-attempt noise variance; RhalfR_{\mathrm{half}}: half-sample reliability. Normality is not required. Unequal half sizes and common completion-rate noise make the data comparison approximate.

Source: Tutorial 4.1, “Check the decomposition with split halves.” The shared player mean produces covariance tau squared. Independent sampling errors add variance within each half without adding covariance between halves. The observed correlation also has sampling uncertainty.

Regression to the mean is arithmetic

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.1 · Player Evaluation and Small Samples

Since Cov⁡(θj,Y‾j)=τ2\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\bar Y_j)=\tau^2, the best linear predictor at a fixed attempt count njn_j is

θ̂j=μ+Rj(y‾j−μ),Rj=Cov⁡(θj,Y‾j)Var⁡(Y‾j)=τ2τ2+σ2/nj. \hat\theta_j=\mu+R_j(\bar y_j-\mu),\qquad R_j=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\bar Y_j)}{\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_j)} =\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2/n_j}.

Regression to the mean in future performance

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.1 · Player Evaluation and Small Samples

Let the future average be Y‾̃j=θj+ε̃j\widetilde{\bar Y}_j=\theta_j+\widetilde\varepsilon_j. Assume the player’s mean is unchanged and future errors have mean zero given ability and past data.

E(Y‾̃j∣Y‾j=y‾j)=E{E(Y‾̃j∣θj,Y‾j=y‾j)∣Y‾j=y‾j}=E(θj∣Y‾j=y‾j)=μ+Rj(y‾j−μ). \begin{aligned} E(\widetilde{\bar Y}_j\mid\bar Y_j=\bar y_j) &=E\{E(\widetilde{\bar Y}_j\mid\theta_j,\bar Y_j=\bar y_j)\mid\bar Y_j=\bar y_j\}\\ &=E(\theta_j\mid\bar Y_j=\bar y_j)\\ &=\mu+R_j(\bar y_j-\mu). \end{aligned}

The final equality uses the normal-normal model. When 0<Rj<10<R_j<1, the expected future average is closer to the population mean, even though the player’s ability has not changed.

jj: player; θj\theta_j: underlying mean; Y‾j\bar Y_j: past random average; y‾j\bar y_j: its observed value; Y‾̃j\widetilde{\bar Y}_j: future average; ε̃j\widetilde\varepsilon_j: future sampling error. μ\mu: population mean; RjR_j: reliability of the past average. A realized future average can still increase or decrease.

The first equality is iterated conditional expectation. The second uses zero-mean future error conditional on ability and past data. The conditional-mean formula in the final line additionally uses the Gaussian hierarchy with fixed population parameters. This separates estimating the player mean from predicting a future realized average.

ML and REML

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.2 · Multilevel Models with Varying Intercepts
  • ML maximizes the full marginal likelihood; variance estimates can be biased downward in finite samples because 𝜷\boldsymbol\beta is estimated from the same data.
  • REML maximizes the likelihood of contrasts free of 𝜷\boldsymbol\beta; it accounts for estimating fixed effects, analogous to dividing a sample variance by n−1n-1. It need not remove all finite-sample bias.

lmer() defaults to REML. Use REML = FALSE to compare models with different fixed effects.

Estimating the prior variance from the data and then conditioning on it is empirical Bayes.

ML: maximum likelihood. REML: restricted maximum likelihood. 𝜷\boldsymbol\beta: fixed-effect coefficient vector. nn: sample size in the sample-variance analogy. τ2\tau^2: population variance of player effects.

From a Gaussian prior to a ridge penalty

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.6 · Regularization as a Prior and Adjusted Plus-Minus

With 𝒚=X𝜷+𝜺\mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon, 𝜺∼N(𝟎,σ2IN)\boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I_N), and βj∼N(0,τ2)\beta_j\sim N(0,\tau^2),

log⁡p(𝜷∣𝒚)=−12σ2‖𝒚−X𝜷‖2−12τ2‖𝜷‖2+const. \log p(\boldsymbol\beta\mid\mathbf y)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2-\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2+\text{const}.

Maximizing it minimizes

‖𝒚−X𝜷‖2+λ‖𝜷‖2,λ=σ2/τ2, \lVert\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2+\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2, \qquad \boxed{\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2},

𝜷̂=(X𝖳X+λI)−1X𝖳𝒚,Cov⁡(𝜷∣𝒚)=σ2(X𝖳X+λI)−1. \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\left(X^{\mathsf T}X+\lambda I\right)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\beta\mid\mathbf y)=\sigma^2\left(X^{\mathsf T}X+\lambda I\right)^{-1}.

NN: observations; pp: penalized coefficients; j=1,…,pj=1,\ldots,p: coefficient index; XX: N×pN\times p design; 𝒚\mathbf y: outcome vector; 𝜷\boldsymbol\beta: coefficient vector. σ2\sigma^2: error variance; τ2\tau^2: independent prior variance. λ\lambda: ridge penalty; ∥⋅∥2\|\cdot\|^2: sum of squares. Three consequences

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.6 · Regularization as a Prior and Adjusted Plus-Minus
  • Prior strength is relative to noise. Larger λ=σ2/τ2\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2 means more shrinkage toward zero.

Identification and lineup collinearity

SGMT 431 · Lecture 4.6 · Regularization as a Prior and Adjusted Plus-Minus

With five home and five away players, every row of XX sums to zero. Adding the same constant to every player effect leaves fitted margins unchanged. The likelihood therefore identifies relative contributions only.

Players who always share the floor also have identical columns. Those data cannot separate their contributions. Players who usually share the floor can still be difficult to distinguish.

A zero-centered Gaussian prior gives a unique ridge estimate and stabilizes relative effects. It supplies assumptions about the split, rather than new lineup information. This is RAPM.

XX: stint-by-player matrix with entries +1+1, −1-1, or 00. RAPM: regularized adjusted plus-minus. Ridge uses a positive penalty. Its fitted coefficients sum to zero for this balanced design and zero-centered prior.

The balanced signed design has X times the all-ones vector equal to zero. Thus even without extra teammate collinearity, unpenalized coefficients require an identifying constraint.
