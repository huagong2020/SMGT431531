4.1 Player Evaluation and the Problem of Small Samples

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • state what a player-evaluation statistic is meant to measure and why an observed average is only an estimate of it;
  • decompose the variance of an observed average into signal and noise and compute a reliability;
  • explain regression to the mean and predict how far a player’s average will regress;
  • fit an expectation model and compute a residual statistic such as completion percentage over expectation (CPOE); and
  • explain why averaging residuals by player assigns credit that may belong elsewhere.

2 Frame the evaluation problem

Let yijy_{ij} be the outcome of pass attempt ii thrown by quarterback jj. For completion, yij∈{0,1}y_{ij}\in\{0,1\}. For expected points added (EPA), yijy_{ij} is a real number.

A raw player statistic is a group mean,

y‾j=1nj∑i=1njyij, \bar y_j=\frac{1}{n_j}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}y_{ij},

where njn_j is the number of attempts. The quantity we care about is not y‾j\bar y_j. It is the player’s underlying ability θj\theta_j, the long-run average that would emerge if the player threw an unlimited number of passes in average circumstances. The sample mean is an estimate of θj\theta_j with error that shrinks as njn_j grows.

qbs <- qb_summary(passes)

The following funnel plot shows every 2023 passer’s completion rate against attempts. The shaded band marks p‾±2p‾(1−p‾)/n\bar p\pm 2\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)/n}, the range a passer of exactly league-average ability would fall into about 95% of the time.

4 Signal plus noise

Write the observed mean as ability plus sampling error,

y‾j=θj+εj,εj∼(0,σ2/nj), \bar y_j=\theta_j+\varepsilon_j, \qquad \varepsilon_j\sim\left(0,\ \sigma^2/n_j\right),

where σ2\sigma^2 is the variance of a single attempt’s outcome. Across the population of players, let abilities vary with mean μ\mu and variance τ2\tau^2. Then

Var⁡(y‾j)=τ2⏟signal+σ2/nj⏟noise. \operatorname{Var}(\bar y_j)=\underbrace{\tau^2}_{\text{signal}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2/n_j}_{\text{noise}}.

The reliability of a player’s average is the share of its variance that is signal,

Rj=τ2τ2+σ2/nj=njnj+σ2/τ2. R_j=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2/n_j}=\frac{n_j}{n_j+\sigma^2/\tau^2}.

The ratio σ2/τ2\sigma^2/\tau^2 is the number of attempts at which reliability equals one half. Beyond that point, the average is more signal than noise.

4.1 Estimate the components from data

For a binary outcome, the within-player variance is approximately p‾(1−p‾)\bar p(1-\bar p). Because Var⁡(y‾j)=τ2+σ2/nj\operatorname{Var}(\bar y_j)=\tau^2+\sigma^2/n_j, a method-of-moments estimate of τ2\tau^2 subtracts the average noise from the observed variance of player means.

eligible <- qbs %>% filter(attempts >= 50)

sigma2_completion <- mean(eligible$completion_rate * (1 - eligible$completion_rate))
tau2_completion <- estimate_tau2_moments(
  eligible$completion_rate,
  eligible$attempts,
  sigma2_completion
)

sigma2_epa <- mean(eligible$epa_sd^2)
tau2_epa <- estimate_tau2_moments(
  eligible$epa_per_attempt,
  eligible$attempts,
  sigma2_epa
)

data.frame(
  statistic = c("completion rate", "EPA per attempt"),
  within_variance = c(sigma2_completion, sigma2_epa),
  between_variance = c(tau2_completion, tau2_epa),
  between_sd = sqrt(c(tau2_completion, tau2_epa)),

4.2 Check the decomposition with split halves

Split halves means dividing each passer’s included attempts into two interleaved samples: attempts 1, 3, 5, and so on form the odd half; attempts 2, 4, 6, and so on form the even half. Calculate a completion rate or mean EPA for each half. Then correlate the two sets of averages across passers, rather than correlating individual attempts within one passer.

To see the connection to reliability, suppose each half contains mm attempts per passer and measures the same underlying mean θj\theta_j:

Y‾j,o=θj+εj,o,Y‾j,e=θj+εj,e. \bar Y_{j,o}=\theta_j+\varepsilon_{j,o}, \qquad \bar Y_{j,e}=\theta_j+\varepsilon_{j,e}.

Here jj indexes passers, oo and ee label the odd and even halves, Y‾\bar Y denotes a random half-sample average, and ε\varepsilon its sampling error. Assume zero conditional error means, errors conditionally independent given θj\theta_j, common error variance σ2/m\sigma^2/m, and between-passer variance Var⁡(θj)=τ2\operatorname{Var}(\theta_j)=\tau^2. The halves share the player mean, but their sampling errors do not covary. Thus,

Cov⁡(Y‾j,o,Y‾j,e)=τ2,Var⁡(Y‾j,o)=Var⁡(Y‾j,e)=τ2+σ2/m. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}(\bar Y_{j,o},\bar Y_{j,e})&=\tau^2,\\ \operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_{j,o})=\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_{j,e})&=\tau^2+\sigma^2/m. \end{aligned}

Dividing the covariance by the product of the two standard deviations gives the population correlation:

Corr⁡(Y‾j,o,Y‾j,e)=τ2τ2+σ2/m=Rhalf, \operatorname{Corr}(\bar Y_{j,o},\bar Y_{j,e}) =\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2/m} =R_{\mathrm{half}},

where RhalfR_{\mathrm{half}} is the reliability of an average over mm attempts. This follows from the variance assumptions without requiring normality. In the data below, half sizes vary, so we use their typical size for an approximate prediction. The observed correlation can differ because of sampling variation, changing context, or dependent attempts. For completion rates, the common noise variance is also an approximation.

split_half <- passes %>%
  group_by(passer) %>%
  filter(n() >= 200) %>%
  mutate(half = if_else(row_number() %% 2 == 1, "odd", "even")) %>%
  group_by(passer, half) %>%
  summarize(
    attempts = n(),
    completion_rate = mean(complete),
    epa = mean(qb_epa),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

wide <- split_half %>%
  tidyr::pivot_wider(
    id_cols = passer,
    names_from = half,
    values_from = c(completion_rate, epa, attempts)

5 Regression to the mean

Reliability is also the slope of the best linear prediction of a player’s underlying mean from the observed average:

θ̂j=μ+Rj(y‾j−μ),Rj=τ2τ2+σ2/nj. \hat\theta_j=\mu+R_j(\bar y_j-\mu), \qquad R_j=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2/n_j}.

Here θj\theta_j is the underlying player mean, θ̂j\hat\theta_j its estimate, y‾j\bar y_j the observed average over njn_j attempts, and μ\mu the population mean. The variances τ2\tau^2 and σ2\sigma^2 describe between-player variation and per-attempt sampling noise. Under the normal-normal model with these population parameters fixed, this predictor is exactly the conditional mean E(θj∣Y‾j=y‾j)E(\theta_j\mid\bar Y_j=\bar y_j). For completion rates, the common-variance Gaussian model is an approximation to binomial sampling.

If the observed average is d=y‾j−μd=\bar y_j-\mu above the population mean, the predicted player mean is only RjdR_jd above it. With stable ability and zero-mean future sampling errors, the Gaussian model also predicts a future average RjdR_jd above the population mean. This is regression to the mean: the expected deviation is smaller even though the player’s ability has not changed. A realized future average can still be higher or lower.

NoteShow the full derivation of regression to the mean

1. Specify the sampling model.

For player jj, let Y‾j\bar Y_j denote the random average before the data are observed, and let y‾j\bar y_j denote its observed value. Write

Y‾j=θj+εj, \bar Y_j=\theta_j+\varepsilon_j,

where εj\varepsilon_j is the error in the sample average, rather than the error on a single attempt. Treat the positive attempt count njn_j as fixed and assume

E(θj)=μ,Var⁡(θj)=τ2,E(εj∣θj)=0,Var⁡(εj∣θj)=σ2/nj. \begin{aligned} E(\theta_j)&=\mu, & \operatorname{Var}(\theta_j)&=\tau^2,\\ E(\varepsilon_j\mid\theta_j)&=0, & \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_j\mid\theta_j)&=\sigma^2/n_j. \end{aligned}

The sampling variance σ2/nj\sigma^2/n_j follows when attempts are conditionally independent and each has conditional variance σ2\sigma^2. Assume finite variances with τ2>0\tau^2>0 and σ2>0\sigma^2>0.

For completion rates, conditionally independent attempts with the same probability θj\theta_j instead give an exact binomial model: Sj∣θj∼Binomial⁡(nj,θj)S_j\mid\theta_j\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n_j,\theta_j) and Y‾j=Sj/nj\bar Y_j=S_j/n_j, where SjS_j is the completion count. Thus E(Y‾j∣θj)=θjE(\bar Y_j\mid\theta_j)=\theta_j and the zero conditional error mean is exact. Its conditional error variance is θj(1−θj)/nj\theta_j(1-\theta_j)/n_j; replacing the numerator by common σ2\sigma^2 is the approximation used here.

2. Compute the mean, variance, and covariance.

By iterated expectation, E(εj)=0E(\varepsilon_j)=0, so E(Y‾j)=μE(\bar Y_j)=\mu. Also,

E(θjεj)=E{θjE(εj∣θj)}=0, E(\theta_j\varepsilon_j) =E\{\theta_jE(\varepsilon_j\mid\theta_j)\}=0,

which gives Cov⁡(θj,εj)=0\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\varepsilon_j)=0. The law of total variance gives Var⁡(εj)=σ2/nj\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_j)=\sigma^2/n_j. Therefore,

Var⁡(Y‾j)=Var⁡(θj)+Var⁡(εj)=τ2+σ2/nj,Cov⁡(θj,Y‾j)=Cov⁡(θj,θj+εj)=τ2. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_j) &=\operatorname{Var}(\theta_j)+\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_j) =\tau^2+\sigma^2/n_j,\\ \operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\bar Y_j) &=\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\theta_j+\varepsilon_j) =\tau^2. \end{aligned}

3. Find the best linear predictor.

Consider a predictor a+bY‾ja+b\bar Y_j, where aa is an intercept and bb is a slope. Choose them to minimize the mean squared prediction error

L(a,b)=E{(θj−a−bY‾j)2}. L(a,b)=E\{(\theta_j-a-b\bar Y_j)^2\}.

For any fixed bb, differentiating with respect to aa gives

∂L∂a=−2E(θj−a−bY‾j)=0,a=μ−bμ. \frac{\partial L}{\partial a} =-2E(\theta_j-a-b\bar Y_j)=0, \qquad a=\mu-b\mu.

Substitute this intercept and expand the centered squared error:

L(μ−bμ,b)=τ2−2bCov⁡(θj,Y‾j)+b2Var⁡(Y‾j). L(\mu-b\mu,b) =\tau^2-2b\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\bar Y_j) +b^2\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_j).

Differentiating with respect to bb yields

−2Cov⁡(θj,Y‾j)+2bVar⁡(Y‾j)=0, -2\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\bar Y_j) +2b\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_j)=0,

so the minimizing slope is

b=Cov⁡(θj,Y‾j)Var⁡(Y‾j)=τ2τ2+σ2/nj=Rj. b=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\theta_j,\bar Y_j)}{\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_j)} =\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2/n_j} =R_j.

The second derivative is 2Var⁡(Y‾j)>02\operatorname{Var}(\bar Y_j)>0, confirming a minimum. After observing Y‾j=y‾j\bar Y_j=\bar y_j, the predictor becomes

θ̂j=μ+Rj(y‾j−μ). \boxed{\hat\theta_j=\mu+R_j(\bar y_j-\mu)}.

Reliability is the regression slope because the covariance equals the signal variance, while the observed variance contains both signal and noise. This linear prediction result uses the stated moments and does not require normality.

4. State when the predictor is the exact conditional mean.

If we additionally assume

θj∼N(μ,τ2),Y‾j∣θj∼N(θj,σ2/nj), \theta_j\sim N(\mu,\tau^2), \qquad \bar Y_j\mid\theta_j\sim N(\theta_j,\sigma^2/n_j),

then θj\theta_j and Y‾j\bar Y_j are jointly normal. Their conditional mean is linear, giving

E(θj∣Y‾j=y‾j)=μ+Rj(y‾j−μ). E(\theta_j\mid\bar Y_j=\bar y_j) =\mu+R_j(\bar y_j-\mu).

Without this additional distributional assumption, the best linear predictor need not equal the conditional mean. The normal-normal posterior is derived in Tutorial 4.4. All displayed conditional formulas here treat μ\mu, τ2\tau^2, and σ2\sigma^2 as fixed; substituting estimates is a separate step.

5. Connect the result to future performance.

Let Y‾̃j=θj+ε̃j\widetilde{\bar Y}_j=\theta_j+\widetilde\varepsilon_j denote an average over future attempts, where the player mean is unchanged and E(ε̃j∣θj,Y‾j)=0E(\widetilde\varepsilon_j\mid\theta_j,\bar Y_j)=0. Iterated conditional expectation gives

E(Y‾̃j∣Y‾j=y‾j)=E{E(Y‾̃j∣θj,Y‾j=y‾j)∣Y‾j=y‾j}=E(θj∣Y‾j=y‾j)=μ+Rj(y‾j−μ). \begin{aligned} E(\widetilde{\bar Y}_j\mid\bar Y_j=\bar y_j) &=E\{E(\widetilde{\bar Y}_j\mid\theta_j,\bar Y_j=\bar y_j)\mid\bar Y_j=\bar y_j\}\\ &=E(\theta_j\mid\bar Y_j=\bar y_j)\\ &=\mu+R_j(\bar y_j-\mu). \end{aligned}

The last equality uses the normal-normal model. Since 0<Rj<10<R_j<1, the expected future average is closer to μ\mu whenever y‾j≠μ\bar y_j\ne\mu. This is an expectation across possible future outcomes, not a guarantee about the next observed average.

Sampling error is only one problem. A screen pass thrown 2 yards and a deep ball thrown 35 yards under pressure have different completion probabilities regardless of the passer. A raw completion rate rewards quarterbacks who throw short.

6.1 An expectation model

The standard remedy is to model the completion probability from the situation and then compare each outcome with its expectation. Let 𝒙i\mathbf{x}_i collect pre-throw features of attempt ii: air yards, pass location, whether the passer was hit, receiver position, down, distance, field position, and formation. A logistic regression gives

p̂i=P(yi=1∣𝒙i)=σ(β0+𝒙i𝖳𝜷),σ(z)=11+e−z. \hat p_i=P(y_i=1\mid\mathbf{x}_i)=\sigma\!\left(\beta_0+\mathbf{x}_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta\right), \qquad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.

The per-attempt residual is completion percentage over expectation,

CPOEi=yi−p̂i, \mathrm{CPOE}_i=y_i-\hat p_i,

and a player’s CPOE is the average of those residuals over their attempts.

7 Residual allocation

CPOE averages residuals by passer. That step contains a modeling assumption: everything the expectation model cannot explain is credited to the quarterback. Consider what else is in a residual:

  • the receiver, who ran the route, created separation, and caught or dropped the ball;
  • the defense, whose coverage and pass rush are only partly captured by qb_hit;
  • the offensive line and scheme; and
  • chance.

A quarterback who throws to an elite receiver against weak defenses accumulates positive residuals that are only partly theirs. Averaging residuals is a residual allocation rule, and it allocates all of the residual to one party.

ImportantTwo separate problems

Sampling noise and credit assignment are different problems with different remedies. Shrinkage addresses the first. Modeling the passer, receiver, and defense together addresses the second. Tutorials 4.2 and 4.3 build a single model that does both.

9 Key takeaways

  • An observed average is ability plus noise, and the noise share depends on attempts.
  • Reliability τ2/(τ2+σ2/n)\tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2/n) is both the signal share and the regression slope toward the mean.
  • An expectation model removes throw difficulty and produces a residual metric such as CPOE.
  • Averaging residuals by player assumes the player owns the whole residual; a multilevel model relaxes that assumption.
