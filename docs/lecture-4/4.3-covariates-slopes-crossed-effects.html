4.3 Covariates, Varying Slopes, and Crossed Effects

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • add level-one covariates to a multilevel model and interpret the random effects that remain;
  • add a level-two covariate and explain what it changes about the passer effects;
  • specify varying slopes and interpret the random-effect covariance;
  • distinguish nested from crossed grouping structures and write each in lme4 syntax;
  • fit a crossed model that attributes each pass to its passer, receiver, and defense; and
  • quantify uncertainty in variance components with a parametric bootstrap.

2 Level-one covariates

Tutorial 4.1 built an expectation model and then averaged residuals by passer. A multilevel model does both steps at once. With level-one covariates 𝒙ij\mathbf x_{ij} describing attempt ii by passer jj,

logit⁡P(yij=1)=β0j+𝒙ij𝖳𝜷,β0j=β0+uj,uj∼N(0,τ2). \operatorname{logit}P(y_{ij}=1)=\beta_{0j}+\mathbf x_{ij}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta, \qquad \beta_{0j}=\beta_0+u_j, \qquad u_j\sim N(0,\tau^2).

The fixed coefficients 𝜷\boldsymbol\beta describe throw difficulty. The random intercept uju_j is the passer’s completion ability after accounting for the throws they attempted. Both are estimated jointly, so the passer effects and the difficulty model inform each other.

intercept_only <- glmer(
  complete ~ 1 + (1 | passer),
  data = passes,
  family = binomial()
)

with_covariates <- glmer(
  complete ~ air_yards_z + qb_hit + pass_location + receiver_position +
    (1 | passer),
  data = passes,
  family = binomial()
)

data.frame(
  model = c("intercept only", "with level-one covariates"),
  passer_sd = c(
    as.data.frame(VarCorr(intercept_only))$sdcor,
    as.data.frame(VarCorr(with_covariates))$sdcor
  )
) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
model passer_sd
intercept only 0.102
with level-one covariates 0.117 
summary(with_covariates)$coefficients %>%
  as.data.frame() %>%
  tibble::rownames_to_column("term") %>%
  select(term, Estimate, `Std. Error`) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
term Estimate Std. Error
(Intercept) 0.871 0.046
air_yards_z -0.627 0.019
qb_hit -0.846 0.058

pass_locationright -0.102 0.047
receiver_positionTE 0.113 0.044
receiver_positionRB 0.064 0.052
receiver_positionother 0.821 0.398

The coefficient on standardized air yards is the change in log-odds of completion for a one-standard-deviation deeper throw, holding the passer and the other covariates fixed. Being hit while throwing is costly.

The passer standard deviation increases once throw difficulty is in the model. That is a feature of the logistic scale: the level-one residual variance is fixed at π2/3\pi^2/3, so explaining part of the outcome with covariates rescales the whole latent scale and inflates the remaining components. The passer effects are also cleaner, because differences in what passers were asked to throw no longer masquerade as differences in passers.

3 A level-two covariate

A level-two covariate describes the passer rather than the attempt. In the level-two equation,

β0j=γ0+γ1wj+uj, \beta_{0j}=\gamma_0+\gamma_1 w_j+u_j,

where wjw_j is a passer-level variable and uju_j is now the deviation from what wjw_j predicts. Examples in practice are age, experience, and draft position. Here we use a contextual variable available in the data: the passer’s average air yards, which measures how aggressively they throw.

passes <- passes %>%
  group_by(passer) %>%
  mutate(passer_mean_air = mean(air_yards)) %>%
  ungroup() %>%
  mutate(passer_air_z = (passer_mean_air - mean(passer_mean_air)) / sd(passer_mean_air))

with_level_two <- glmer(
  complete ~ air_yards_z + passer_air_z + qb_hit + pass_location +
    receiver_position + (1 | passer),
  data = passes,
  family = binomial()
)

summary(with_level_two)$coefficients %>%
  as.data.frame() %>%
  tibble::rownames_to_column("term") %>%
  filter(term %in% c("air_yards_z", "passer_air_z")) %>%
  select(term, Estimate, `Std. Error`) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
term Estimate Std. Error
air_yards_z -0.630 0.019
passer_air_z 0.035 0.020

The level-one coefficient says a deeper throw is harder for a given passer. The level-two coefficient asks whether passers who throw deeper on average complete more or fewer passes at a given depth. A coefficient near zero says aggressiveness itself carries no penalty once the depth of each throw is in the model.

4 Varying slopes

So far every passer shares the same air-yards coefficient. Perhaps some passers lose accuracy faster with depth than others. A varying-slope model lets each passer have both an intercept and a slope:

logit⁡P(yij=1)=β0j+β1jxij+⋯,(β0jβ1j)=(β0β1)+(u0ju1j),(u0ju1j)∼N(𝟎,Σ), \operatorname{logit}P(y_{ij}=1)=\beta_{0j}+\beta_{1j}x_{ij}+\cdots, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}\beta_{0j}\\ \beta_{1j}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\ \beta_1\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}u_{0j}\\ u_{1j}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}u_{0j}\\ u_{1j}\end{pmatrix} \sim N\!\left(\mathbf 0,\ \Sigma\right),

with

Σ=(τ02ρτ0τ1ρτ0τ1τ12). \Sigma= \begin{pmatrix} \tau_0^2 & \rho\tau_0\tau_1\\ \rho\tau_0\tau_1 & \tau_1^2 \end{pmatrix}.

The correlation ρ\rho asks whether passers who are better on average (large u0ju_{0j}) are also better or worse on deep throws (large or small u1ju_{1j}).

varying_slopes <- glmer(
  complete ~ air_yards_z + qb_hit + pass_location + receiver_position +
    (1 + air_yards_z | passer),
  data = passes,
  family = binomial()
)

as.data.frame(VarCorr(varying_slopes)) %>%
  select(grp, var1, var2, sdcor) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
grp var1 var2 sdcor
passer (Intercept) NA 0.112
passer air_yards_z NA 0.087
passer (Intercept) air_yards_z 0.887

The slope standard deviation is small relative to the fixed slope of about −0.63-0.63. The correlation between intercepts and slopes is strongly positive: passers with higher intercepts also have less negative slopes, so the passers who complete more short throws hold up better on deep ones. A likelihood-ratio comparison with the varying-intercept model shows how much evidence the data provide for passer-specific depth effects.

anova(with_covariates, varying_slopes) %>%
  as.data.frame() %>%
  select(npar, AIC, BIC, logLik, Chisq, `Pr(>Chisq)`) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
npar AIC BIC logLik Chisq Pr(>Chisq)

4.1 Passer-specific curves

top_passers <- passes %>%
  count(passer, sort = TRUE) %>%
  slice_head(n = 6) %>%
  pull(passer) %>%
  as.character()

coefficients <- coef(varying_slopes)$passer %>%
  tibble::rownames_to_column("passer") %>%
  filter(passer %in% top_passers)

air_grid <- seq(-10, 40, by = 1)
air_z <- (air_grid - mean(passes$air_yards)) / sd(passes$air_yards)

curves <- lapply(seq_len(nrow(coefficients)), function(r) {
  data.frame(
    passer = coefficients$passer[[r]],
    air_yards = air_grid,
    probability = plogis(coefficients$`(Intercept)`[[r]] + coefficients$air_yards_z[[r]] * air_z)
  )
}) %>%
  bind_rows()

ggplot(curves, aes(air_yards, probability, color = passer)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 0.9) +
  labs(x = "Air yards", y = "Completion probability", color = NULL) +
  theme_minimal() +
  theme(legend.position = "bottom")

The coef() function returns β0+u0j\beta_0+u_{0j} and β1+u1j\beta_1+u_{1j} for each passer, that is, the passer-specific intercepts and slopes after shrinkage.

WarningSingular fits

When a slope variance is estimated at or near zero, lme4 reports a singular fit. The model has not failed; the data simply contain no evidence that the slopes vary. Simplify the random-effect structure or, as in Tutorial 4.5, use a Bayesian model whose prior keeps the variance away from exactly zero.

5 Nested and crossed structures

Grouping factors can relate to each other in two ways.

Nested. Each level of the inner factor belongs to exactly one level of the outer factor. Attempts within games within a passer are nested: a game-by-passer combination belongs to one passer. In lme4, (1 | passer / game_id) expands to (1 | passer) + (1 | passer:game_id).

Crossed. Levels of one factor combine with many levels of another. Each pass has a passer, a receiver, and a defense, and the same receiver catches passes from several passers while the same defense faces every passer it plays. Crossed effects are written as separate terms, (1 | passer) + (1 | receiver) + (1 | defteam).

The crossed model is

logit⁡P(yi=1)=β0+𝒙i𝖳𝜷+uq(i)+vr(i)+dk(i), \operatorname{logit}P(y_i=1)=\beta_0+\mathbf x_i^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\beta+u_{q(i)}+v_{r(i)}+d_{k(i)},

where q(i)q(i), r(i)r(i), and k(i)k(i) index the passer, receiver, and defense on attempt ii, and

uq∼N(0,τq2),vr∼N(0,τr2),dk∼N(0,τd2). u_q\sim N(0,\tau_q^2),\qquad v_r\sim N(0,\tau_r^2),\qquad d_k\sim N(0,\tau_d^2).

Every pass now contributes to three effects at once. This is the multilevel answer to the residual-allocation problem: the residual is shared among the parties according to how consistently each one is associated with success.

crossed <- glmer(
  complete ~ air_yards_z + qb_hit + pass_location + receiver_position +
    (1 | passer) + (1 | receiver) + (1 | defteam),
  data = passes,
  family = binomial()
)

as.data.frame(VarCorr(crossed)) %>%
  select(grp, sdcor) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
grp sdcor
receiver 0.146
passer 0.095
defteam 0.117

The receiver standard deviation is at least as large as the passer standard deviation. On completion, who is catching the ball matters at least as much as who is throwing it, a fact a passer-only model cannot express.

For EPA the defense component is smaller and the residual dominates. The variance components differ by outcome because completion and EPA measure different things.

6 Uncertainty in variance components

Standard errors for fixed effects come from the curvature of the log-likelihood and are reported by summary(). Variance components are different: their sampling distributions are skewed and bounded at zero, so a symmetric standard error is a poor summary. The standard remedy is a parametric bootstrap:

  1. simulate a new response vector from the fitted model, drawing new random effects and new residuals;
  2. refit the model to the simulated data;
  3. record the statistics of interest; and
  4. repeat and read off percentiles.

The results below were precomputed with bootMer() for the EPA model, using 200 replicates.

bootstrap <- results$bootstrap$statistics
variance_names <- c("passer", "receiver", "defteam")

bootstrap[, variance_names] %>%
  as.data.frame() %>%
  tidyr::pivot_longer(everything(), names_to = "component", values_to = "sd") %>%
  group_by(component) %>%
  summarize(
    estimate = results$bootstrap$observed[[first(component)]],
    lower_95 = quantile(sd, 0.025),
    upper_95 = quantile(sd, 0.975),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
component estimate lower_95 upper_95
defteam 0.053 0.000 0.078
passer 0.097 0.054 0.131
receiver 0.112 0.063 0.145 
bootstrap[, variance_names] %>%
  as.data.frame() %>%
  tidyr::pivot_longer(everything(), names_to = "component", values_to = "sd") %>%
  ggplot(aes(sd, fill = component)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 30, alpha = 0.6, position = "identity") +
  labs(x = "Bootstrap standard deviation", y = "Replicates", fill = NULL) +
  theme_minimal()

The defense interval reaches close to zero: one season of EPA cannot establish stable differences between defenses on this outcome. The passer and receiver components are clearly positive.

NoteTwo kinds of uncertainty about a player effect

The bootstrap above resimulates new random effects in every replicate (use.u = FALSE), so it describes the sampling distribution of the variance components, not of any particular passer’s effect. Uncertainty about one passer’s effect, given the variance components, is the conditional standard deviation from Tutorial 4.2. Tutorial 4.5 obtains both at once from a posterior distribution.

7 Practice

  1. Write the crossed model for EPA with passer, receiver, and defense effects in level-one and level-two form.
  2. Why does adding level-one covariates change the passer variance?
  3. When is (1 | passer / game_id) appropriate, and what does it add relative to (1 | passer)?
  4. If the receiver standard deviation exceeds the passer standard deviation, does that mean receivers are more important than passers for winning?

8 Key takeaways

  • Level-one covariates turn the random intercept into an “over expectation” effect estimated jointly with the expectation model.
  • Varying slopes let a covariate’s effect differ by player, with a covariance that links intercepts and slopes.
  • Crossed random effects share each play’s residual among passer, receiver, and defense.
  • A parametric bootstrap provides honest intervals for variance components.
