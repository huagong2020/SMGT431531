3.1 Soccer Shot Prediction and a Baseline

Predict a goal probability and establish a simple comparison

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • define what a goal probability means;
  • identify information that would reveal the answer too early;
  • explain why observations should be separated by match and time;
  • fit and interpret a logistic-regression baseline; and
  • compare shot-feature tables with player-location maps.

2 Frame the prediction problem

NoteBefore running code

Lecture 2 used logistic regression and separate training, validation, and test data. We keep that workflow. First establish a simple comparison model, then ask whether neural networks improve its predictions.

For shot ii, let

Yi={1,if the shot becomes a goal,0,otherwise. Y_i = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if the shot becomes a goal},\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}

We want a probability for each shot, using its available features 𝒙i\mathbf{x}_i:

p̂i=P(Yi=1∣𝒙i), \hat p_i = P(Y_i=1\mid \mathbf{x}_i),

This is expected goals, or xG. A value of 0.20 means a 20% goal chance. If 100 comparable shots each receive 0.20, about 20 should become goals. Agreement between predicted chances and observed frequencies is called calibration.

Each observation is one shot that was taken. We are not predicting whether a team will create a shot. Add shot probabilities to get an expected goal total: 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.50 expected goals, even though the actual total must be a whole number.

3 Use only pre-shot information

Features are the inputs used to predict. They must be known when the shot is taken: location, body part, pressure, and player positions in a freeze frame, a snapshot at that moment.

Leakage means using information unavailable at prediction time. Examples include:

  • shot outcome (Goal, Saved, or Blocked);
  • the ending location of the ball;
  • a post-shot expected-goals value; or
  • any statistic updated after the shot.
WarningLeakage can produce impressive but useless accuracy

Always state the prediction moment first. Then audit every feature by asking, “Would this value have been available at that moment?”

Only about 11% are goals. Always choosing “no goal” would be about 89% accurate while failing to distinguish easy chances from difficult ones.

Our simplest probability reference gives every shot the training goal rate, about 0.103. It captures the overall frequency but cannot rank chances. Choosing a “no goal” label is different from claiming the goal probability is zero.

TipCheckpoint: same labels, different forecasts

Two models report goal probabilities of .01 and .20 for a shot that becomes a goal. Both predict “no goal” at a .50 threshold. Which forecast should receive the smaller penalty, and why?

Check your reasoning

The .20 forecast gave more probability to the observed outcome. Its log loss is −log⁡(.20)=1.609-\log(.20)=1.609, compared with −log⁡(.01)=4.605-\log(.01)=4.605. Accuracy treats the two predictions identically. We will train the networks using this probability-sensitive loss.

Logistic regression and the MLP use the following 10 shot features. The table describes their values before standardization; a binary indicator uses 1 for yes and 0 for no.

Feature What it tells us
shot_x Shot location along the pitch’s length, from 0 to 120. The attacking goal is at 120.
shot_y Shot location across the pitch’s width, from 0 to 80; 40 is the middle.
distance Straight-line distance from the shot to the goal center, in StatsBomb coordinate units.
angle How wide the opening between the goalposts looks from the shot location, in radians. Larger means a wider opening.
header 1 if the shot is taken with the head; 0 for other body parts.
set_piece 1 if the recorded shot type is not “Open Play,” such as a direct free kick; 0 for “Open Play.” Penalties are excluded from this dataset.
under_pressure 1 if StatsBomb marks the shooter as under defensive pressure when shooting; 0 otherwise.
first_time 1 if the player shoots on their first touch, without a controlling touch beforehand; 0 otherwise.
visible_attackers Number of attacking teammates recorded with locations in the shot’s freeze frame, excluding the shooter.
visible_defenders Number of opponents recorded with locations in that frame, including the goalkeeper.

The two counts describe the recorded frame, not every player on the pitch or only players between the ball and goal. Also, set_piece uses the shot type, not whether an earlier corner or free kick started the attack. The CNN receives four maps of player presence instead of these 10 values.

5 Construct interpretable shot features

Is the pitch 120 by 80 meters? No. StatsBomb uses a standard 120×80120\times80 coordinate grid: xx runs along the length and yy across the width. These are provider coordinate units, not physical meters; the grid does not state a stadium’s actual dimensions. The attacking goal center is (120,40)(120,40), with posts at (120,36)(120,36) and (120,44)(120,44), as shown in StatsBomb’s pitch-coordinate specification (Appendix 2).

Shot distance is

di=(120−xi)2+(40−yi)2. d_i = \sqrt{(120-x_i)^2+(40-y_i)^2}.

Shot angle measures how wide the goal opening looks from the shooter. Calculate the directions to the posts, then take their difference:

θi=|atan2(44−yi,120−xi)−atan2(36−yi,120−xi)|. \theta_i = \left| \operatorname{atan2}(44-y_i,120-x_i) - \operatorname{atan2}(36-y_i,120-x_i) \right|.

atan2 gives a direction angle. Closer shots and wider goal openings generally offer better chances.

Distance uses the provider’s coordinate units, not meters. Angles use radians. From (108,40)(108,40), distance is 122+02=12\sqrt{12^2+0^2}=12 and angle is 2arctan⁡(4/12)≈0.6442\arctan(4/12)\approx0.644 radians. The helper replaces angle differences above π\pi with 2π−θ2\pi-\theta to keep the smaller opening angle.

6 Split by match and chronology

Shots in one match share teams, tactics, and conditions. Keep a whole match in one data group, rather than scattering its shots across groups. We sort matches by date, then assign them to training, validation, and test sets.

shots$metadata %>%
  distinct(match_id, match_date, split) %>%
  group_by(split) %>%
  summarize(
    first_date = min(match_date),
    last_date = max(match_date),
    matches = n(),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) %>%
  knitr::kable()
split first_date last_date matches
test 2022-12-05 2022-12-18 11
train 2022-11-20 2022-12-01 44
validation 2022-12-02 2022-12-05 9

Training fits the model. Validation chooses settings and when to stop. Test measures final performance after those choices are fixed.

Validation and test both include December 5, 2022 matches. No match appears in both, but the dates overlap. This checks later matches within the same tournament. It does not establish performance in another season.

7 Fit a logistic baseline

A baseline is a simple model to compare against. Logistic regression multiplies each feature by a fitted coefficient and adds an intercept. Its score ηi\eta_i is the logit, or log odds:

log⁡(pi1−pi)=β0+β1xi1+⋯+βpxip. \log\left(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1}+\cdots+\beta_px_{ip}.

Sigmoid converts that score into a goal probability:

pi=σ(ηi)=11+e−ηi. p_i = \sigma(\eta_i) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta_i}}.

Standardization subtracts each column’s training mean and divides by its training standard deviation. We apply it to all 10 columns, including 0/1 indicators, and reuse those training values everywhere. A constant column uses scale 1. The MLP uses exactly the same prepared inputs.

Show input preparation and logistic-regression code 
standardized <- sweep(
  sweep(shots$tabular, 2, shots$feature_center, "-"),
  2,
  shots$feature_scale,
  "/"
)

baseline_data <- as.data.frame(standardized)
names(baseline_data) <- shots$feature_names
baseline_data$goal <- shots$target

training_rows <- shots$metadata$split == "train"
validation_rows <- shots$metadata$split == "validation"

logistic_fit <- glm(
  goal ~ .,
  data = baseline_data[training_rows, ],
  family = binomial()
)

baseline_probability <- predict(
  logistic_fit,
  newdata = baseline_data[validation_rows, ],
  type = "response"
)
summary(logistic_fit)$coefficients %>%

A positive coefficient raises the fitted goal probability when that input increases and the others stay fixed. A negative coefficient lowers it. Each coefficient refers to a one-standard-deviation increase because the inputs are standardized. It describes an association, not a cause.

NoteInterpreting standardized indicators

For a binary indicator, changing from 0 to 1 changes its standardized value by 1/sjtrain1/s_j^{\text{train}}. Its corresponding odds ratio is therefore exp⁡(βj/sjtrain)\exp(\beta_j/s_j^{\text{train}}), not simply exp⁡(βj)\exp(\beta_j). Distance and angle are derived from location, so interpret individual coefficients carefully when these inputs are correlated.

8 Establish the validation benchmark

The constant forecast estimates one parameter: the goal frequency in the training data. Compare it with logistic regression on the validation matches.

training_goal_rate <- mean(shots$target[training_rows])
validation_target <- shots$target[validation_rows]

bind_rows(
  evaluate_probabilities(
    validation_target,
    rep(training_goal_rate, sum(validation_rows))
  ) %>% mutate(model = "Training-rate forecast"),
  evaluate_probabilities(validation_target, baseline_probability) %>%
    mutate(model = "Logistic regression")
) %>%
  select(model, log_loss, brier) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
model log_loss brier
Training-rate forecast 0.352 0.100
Logistic regression 0.310 0.094

Read log loss and Brier score as probability-error scores: lower is better. Tutorial 3.6 applies these scores, checks calibration, and presents the final test comparison.

Use this validation comparison during development. Do not calculate test scores while choosing features or tuning settings.

9 Practice

  1. Why is accuracy misleading when only about 11% of shots are goals?
  2. Give one example of target leakage in this problem.
  3. Why do we compute feature means and standard deviations using the training set only?
  4. Why compare a neural network with logistic regression?
TipShow answers
  1. A model that always predicts no goal would be about 89% accurate while providing no useful ranking or probability estimates.
  2. The recorded shot outcome, the ball’s ending location, or any post-shot xG value would reveal information unavailable at the prediction moment.
  3. Validation and test data should check the training choices, not help determine the scaling. Reusing training statistics also matches how new shots would be processed.
  4. It checks whether the neural network adds enough predictive value to justify its extra complexity.

10 Key takeaways

  • Define the shot moment, observed outcome, and prediction before fitting a model.
  • Keep complete matches together and preserve time order when possible.
  • A simple logistic baseline is part of a strong neural-network analysis.
  • One shot can become a row of features for an MLP or player-location maps for a CNN.
