7.2 The Fourth-Down Decision Tree

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

  • Separate the chance of an outcome from the value of the situation that follows it.
  • Compare a field goal, a conversion attempt, and a punt on the same scale.
  • Keep the win-probability perspective fixed when possession changes.

2 Set up the decision

Our team has the ball and trails 20–22 on fourth-and-2 at the opponent’s 40. The line to gain is the 38. The three actions are to go for the first down, attempt a field goal, or punt. The field-goal distance is 57 yards in the model because we assume 40 yards to the goal line, 10 through the end zone, and a hold 7 yards behind the line of scrimmage.

Assume two scrimmage snaps remain, including this fourth down. The model folds the kickoff after a made field goal into the transition to the final snap and omits unusual outcomes such as a return touchdown. The probabilities are hypothetical. Evaluate every branch from our team’s perspective.

For now, take the post-play win probabilities in the last column as given. Tutorial 7.4 computes them from the final snap.

Fourth-down action Outcome in the model Probability given the action Our win probability afterward
Field goal Make it and lead by one before the opponent’s final snap 0.40 0.90
Field goal Miss it and trail by two with the opponent able to kneel 0.60 0
Go Gain five yards and retain the ball at the opponent’s 35 0.55 0.65
Go Turn the ball over on downs with the opponent able to kneel 0.45 0
Punt Give the opponent possession and a final kneel 1.00 0

The three zero-value outcomes may leave the opponent at different yard lines. We can group them here because each leads to a final kneel and the same terminal result in the model.

3 Compare on a common scale

The fourth-down state is fixed, so write Q(a)Q(a) for the action value Q(s,a)Q(s,a) from Tutorial 7.1. Here the payoff attached to a branch is our win probability from the state it leads to. Multiply each branch probability by its continuation value, then sum within the action.

Q(field goal)=0.40(0.90)+0.60(0)=0.3600,Q(go)=0.55(0.65)+0.45(0)=0.3575,Q(punt)=1.00(0)=0. \begin{aligned} Q(\text{field goal}) &= 0.40(0.90)+0.60(0)=0.3600,\\ Q(\text{go}) &= 0.55(0.65)+0.45(0)=0.3575,\\ Q(\text{punt}) &= 1.00(0)=0. \end{aligned}

The model gives the 57-yard field goal a 0.25 percentage-point advantage over going for it, because 100(0.3600−0.3575)=0.25100(0.3600-0.3575)=0.25. Converting is more likely than making the long kick, but a conversion leaves the offense trailing and still needing a score.

In a fuller tree, a conversion could gain two yards, five yards, or enough for a touchdown. Each outcome can have a different continuation value. A two-branch tree can still be used if its success value is the average over all the states reached after success, weighted by their probabilities conditional on success.

NotePossession and perspective

If a model returns the probability that the team with the ball wins, its output after a turnover refers to the opponent. In a win/loss model, subtract the opponent’s win probability from one to obtain ours. If a tie is possible and receives payoff zero for both teams, subtract the tie probability as well.

4 Practice

Suppose the same conversion attempt still succeeds 55% of the time. Given success, two-thirds of the time it sets up a state worth 0.60 and one-third of the time a state worth 0.75. A failed attempt still has value zero. Calculate the action value of going for it.

TipCheck your calculation

The conditional value after success is (2/3)(0.60)+(1/3)(0.75)=0.65(2/3)(0.60)+(1/3)(0.75)=0.65. The action value is 0.55(0.65)=0.35750.55(0.65)=0.3575.
