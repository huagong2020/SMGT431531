2.1 NFL Win Probability and a Logistic Baseline

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After completing this tutorial, you should be able to:

  1. define the observational unit and target for an in-game model;
  2. identify common forms of sports-data leakage;
  3. explain the logistic link mathematically;
  4. create game-grouped development and validation samples; and
  5. evaluate a probability model with Brier score, log loss, and calibration.

2 Frame the prediction problem

For state ii, let

Yi={1if the home team eventually wins,0otherwise. Y_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the home team eventually wins,}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}

The unit of observation is a game state immediately before a play. The model estimates

p̂i=P(Yi=1∣Xi), \hat p_i=P(Y_i=1\mid X_i),

where XiX_i contains only information available at that moment.

4 Avoid leakage

Do not use variables that would be unavailable at prediction time. Examples include the final score, post-play score when predicting the pre-play state, season ratings calculated with future games, or any existing win-probability estimate used as a predictor.

Randomly splitting rows is also leakage. One NFL game contributes many states with the same eventual outcome. If states from one game appear in both training and validation sets, the validation score is overly optimistic.

6 Logistic regression

Logistic regression models the log odds of a home win:

log⁡(pi1−pi)=β0+β1xi1+⋯+βpxip. \log\left(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}\right) =\beta_0+\beta_1x_{i1}+\cdots+\beta_px_{ip}.

The inverse transformation converts log odds to a probability:

pi=11+exp⁡(−ηi),ηi=Xiβ. p_i=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\eta_i)}, \qquad \eta_i=X_i\beta.

For independent binary outcomes, the likelihood is

ℒ(β)=∏i=1npiyi(1−pi)1−yi. \mathcal L(\beta)= \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}.

It is more convenient to maximize the log likelihood,

ℓ(β)=∑i=1n[yilog(pi)+(1−yi)log(1−pi)], \ell(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[y_i\log(p_i)+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right],

or equivalently minimize negative log likelihood. The estimated coefficients satisfy

β̂=arg⁡maxβℓ(β). \hat\beta=\arg\max_\beta \ell(\beta).

For coefficient βj\beta_j, holding other variables fixed, a one-unit increase in xjx_j multiplies the odds by exp⁡(βj)\exp(\beta_j). With an interaction such as score difference multiplied by time remaining, neither main-effect coefficient has one constant interpretation across every game state; the marginal log-odds effect depends on the interacting variable.

6.1 Numerical example

Suppose a simplified model produces a linear predictor of η=0.80\eta=0.80 for a home team that is leading late in the game. Then

p=11+e−0.80=0.690. p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-0.80}}=0.690.

The model assigns a 69.0% home-win probability—not a guaranteed home win.

eta <- 0.80
1 / (1 + exp(-eta))
[1] 0.6899745

For binary outcomes, the Brier score is

Brier=1n∑i=1n(yi−p̂i)2, \text{Brier}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat p_i)^2,

and log loss is

−1n∑i=1n[yilog(p̂i)+(1−yi)log(1−p̂i)]. -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[y_i\log(\hat p_i)+(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\right].

Both metrics should be minimized.

9 Examine calibration

9.1 What calibration means

A model is calibrated when states assigned probability 0.70 lead to home wins approximately 70% of the time. Formally, writing p̂(X)\hat p(X) for the model’s forecast, calibration is the requirement

P{Y=1∣p̂(X)=p}=pfor every p∈[0,1]. P\{Y=1\mid \hat p(X)=p\}=p \qquad\text{for every } p\in[0,1].

Read that carefully: it conditions on what the model said. It asks whether the number the model produces means what it claims to mean. That is a different question from either of the two you have already met.

ImportantWhy calibration matters more here than in most classification problems

In most applications a probability is an intermediate quantity and only the final label is used. In win probability the number is the product. It is printed on a broadcast graphic, drives a fourth-down decision model, sizes a position, and is quoted back at the analyst on Monday. A model that says 90% when the truth is 70% is wrong in a way no downstream threshold can repair.

9.2 Read the plot

Each point is a bin of states with similar predictions. The horizontal position is the average forecast in that bin; the vertical position is the fraction that actually ended in a home win. Perfect calibration puts every point on the 45-degree line.

  • A point below the line means the model promised more than it delivered: overconfident in that range.
  • A point above the line means the outcome happened more often than predicted: underconfident.
  • A curve that is systematically flatter than the diagonal — high predictions too high and low predictions too low — is the classic signature of an overconfident model, and it is the pattern recalibration can repair.

Point size matters as much as position. A bin holding twenty states will bounce around the line from sampling noise alone, so read the large bins first and treat small ones as suggestive. Tutorial 2.6 quantifies this and shows how to fix a miscalibrated model.

logistic_calibration <- calibration_table(
  validation_data$home_win,
  validation_probability

11 Key takeaways

  • Win probability is a probability-estimation problem, not only a classification problem.
  • Complete games—not rows—must be assigned to resamples.
  • A future season provides a realistic final test.
  • Logistic regression is an essential benchmark for more complex models.
