2.6 Model Evaluation and Calibration

Choosing a useful in-game win probability model

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

By the end of this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • compare probability models on a future NFL season;
  • define a proper scoring rule and prove that the Brier score and log loss are strictly proper;
  • prove that accuracy is not a proper scoring rule and explain what that costs;
  • distinguish discrimination, accuracy, and calibration, and show that the first two cannot detect a calibration failure;
  • diagnose calibration by segment and repair it with Platt scaling or isotonic regression;
  • diagnose performance at different stages of a game;
  • judge whether a difference between two models is larger than its uncertainty; and
  • design a defensible final modeling workflow.

2 The final test is a future season

The models were developed using 2022 games. This tutorial evaluates them on 2023 games, which approximates the real task: predicting games that occur after model development.

All observations from a game remain in the same split. A random row split would put early states from a game in training and later states from that same game in testing, producing an unrealistically easy evaluation.

What metrics should be used to evaluate win probability models? Previously, performance has been measured using Brier score and log loss. But what about accuracy? Is prediction accuracy a reliable metric for evaluating probability models?

3 Model evaluation: Two scoring rules

A scoring rule in probability measures the accuracy of a probabilistic forecast by assigning a numerical penalty or reward based on a prediction and the actual outcome that occurs.

For a binary outcome yi∈{0,1}y_i\in\{0,1\} and probability pip_i, the Brier score is

Brier=1n∑i=1n(yi−pi)2,\text{Brier}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-p_i)^2,

and log loss is

Log loss=−1n∑i=1n[yilog(pi)+(1−yi)log(1−pi)].\text{Log loss}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[y_i\log(p_i)+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right].

Both are minimized by honest probabilities, and log loss penalizes confident mistakes more severely. The next section explains precisely what “minimized by honest probabilities” means, why these two rules have that property, and why accuracy does not.

4 What makes a scoring rule proper

The question that decides whether a rule can be used to estimate probabilities is this. Suppose a forecaster knows the true probability q=P(Y=1)q=P(Y=1). Does reporting p=qp=q minimize the penalty the forecaster expects to pay? That expected penalty is

S‾(p,q)=qS(p,1)+(1−q)S(p,0).\bar S(p,q)=q\,S(p,1)+(1-q)\,S(p,0).

A rule is proper when q∈arg⁡min⁡pS‾(p,q)q\in\arg\min_p\bar S(p,q) for every q∈[0,1]q\in[0,1], and strictly proper when qq is the only minimizer. Strict propriety is the property that matters: it means every misreport is punished, so the forecaster’s best strategy is to state what it actually believes.

4.1 The Brier score is strictly proper

With S(p,y)=(y−p)2S(p,y)=(y-p)^2,

S‾(p,q)=q(1−p)2+(1−q)p2.\bar S(p,q)=q(1-p)^2+(1-q)p^2.

Expanding gives q−2pq+p2q-2pq+p^2, and completing the square yields an identity worth remembering:

S‾(p,q)=q(1−q)⏟irreducible+(p−q)2⏟cost of misreporting.\bar S(p,q)=\underbrace{q(1-q)}_{\text{irreducible}} +\underbrace{(p-q)^2}_{\text{cost of misreporting}}.

The first term is the variance of a Bernoulli outcome. No forecaster can avoid it, and it does not depend on the forecast. The second term is zero exactly when p=qp=q and strictly positive otherwise. Therefore p=qp=q is the unique minimizer, and the Brier score is strictly proper.

Calculus confirms it:

∂S‾∂p=−2q(1−p)+2(1−q)p=2(p−q),∂2S‾∂p2=2>0.\frac{\partial\bar S}{\partial p}=-2q(1-p)+2(1-q)p=2(p-q), \qquad \frac{\partial^2\bar S}{\partial p^2}=2>0.

The derivative vanishes only at p=qp=q, and the function is convex.

4.2 Log loss is strictly proper

With S(p,y)=−[ylogp+(1−y)log(1−p)]S(p,y)=-\left[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)\right],

S‾(p,q)=−[qlogp+(1−q)log(1−p)].\bar S(p,q)=-\left[q\log p+(1-q)\log(1-p)\right].

Subtract the penalty an honest forecaster pays, S‾(q,q)\bar S(q,q):

S‾(p,q)−S‾(q,q)=qlog⁡qp+(1−q)log⁡1−q1−p=DKL(q∥p),\bar S(p,q)-\bar S(q,q) =q\log\frac{q}{p}+(1-q)\log\frac{1-q}{1-p} =D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p),

the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the true Bernoulli(q)(q) to the reported Bernoulli(p)(p). Gibbs’ inequality gives DKL(q∥p)≥0D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p)\ge 0 with equality if and only if p=qp=q. Log loss is therefore strictly proper, and

S‾(p,q)=H(q)⏟entropy+DKL(q∥p)⏟cost of misreporting\bar S(p,q)=\underbrace{H(q)}_{\text{entropy}} +\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p)}_{\text{cost of misreporting}}

has the same “irreducible plus penalty” structure as the Brier score. The two rules differ in how steeply they punish: the Brier penalty grows quadratically in p−qp-q and is bounded by 1, while the log-loss penalty grows without bound as p→0p\rightarrow 0 with y=1y=1.

4.3 Accuracy is not a proper scoring rule

Accuracy first converts the forecast to a label and then checks the label. Using threshold tt, the forecaster predicts 1 when p≥tp\ge t, so as a loss

Sacc(p,y)=𝟏{𝟏(p≥t)≠y}.S_{\mathrm{acc}}(p,y)=\mathbf 1\left\{\mathbf 1(p\ge t)\ne y\right\}.

Taking the expectation over Y∼Bernoulli(q)Y\sim\text{Bernoulli}(q),

S‾acc(p,q)={1−q,p≥t,q,p<t.\bar S_{\mathrm{acc}}(p,q)= \begin{cases} 1-q, & p\ge t,\\[2pt] q, & p<t. \end{cases}

The forecast enters only through which side of tt it falls on. The expected score is a two-valued step function of pp, not a function with a minimum at qq. Two distinct failures follow.

Failure 1: it is not strictly proper. Let t=0.5t=0.5 and q=0.70q=0.70. Every report in [0.5,1][0.5,1] earns the same expected error 1−q=0.301-q=0.30. Reporting the true value 0.70, reporting 0.51, and reporting 1.00 are exactly equally good. Accuracy cannot distinguish an honest forecaster from a maximally overconfident one, so it creates no incentive to be calibrated. An infinite set of reports ties with the truth.

Failure 2: at any threshold other than 0.50.5 it is not even proper. The expected error is smaller on the “predict 1” side whenever q>1/2q>1/2, so the accuracy-optimal report satisfies p≥tp\ge t if and only if q>1/2q>1/2. Honest reporting puts p≥tp\ge t exactly when q≥tq\ge t. These two rules agree only when t=1/2t=1/2.

ImportantThe practical consequence

A model selected or tuned on accuracy is being rewarded for pushing predictions toward 0 and 1, not for getting them right. In win probability that is exactly the wrong incentive: the number itself is the product.

Three features of the model performance table above deserve discussion, and none of them is “the newest algorithm won.”

The logistic baseline is the strongest course model. It has both the lowest Brier score and the lowest log loss of the seven models built in this lecture. Five tutorials of tree ensembles did not beat a generalized linear model with one interaction term. That is a real and common result, not a mistake in the tutorials. The predictors are few, mostly continuous, and related to the outcome in a smooth and close-to-monotone way — score difference and time remaining behave almost exactly as a linear log-odds model assumes. Tree ensembles earn their keep when there are many predictors, sharp thresholds, high-order interactions, or categorical variables with many levels. This problem has none of those in abundance.

The lesson is the one stated in the lecture overview: a complex model must be justified against a baseline on held-out data, and here the justification fails. Reporting that honestly is the analysis. Choosing the boosted model anyway, because it is more impressive, is not.

The published nflverse model beats everything. It is trained on many more seasons, uses a richer state representation, and has been refined over years. It sets a realistic ceiling for what these eight predictors and one development season can achieve, and it shows how much of the remaining gap is data and feature engineering rather than algorithm choice.

The differences among the course models are small. The seven models span roughly .03 in log loss on 8,358 states from 272 games. Before declaring a winner, ask whether that gap is larger than the uncertainty. A bootstrap that resamples whole games, not individual states, is the right tool; Tutorial 3.6 uses the same idea for soccer shots.

set.seed(431)
game_ids <- unique(predictions$game_id)

7 Repairing a miscalibrated model

When calibration fails, it is often fixable after training, without refitting the model. Two standard maps from a raw score to a calibrated probability:

  • Platt scaling fits a one-variable logistic regression of the outcome on the raw log odds, P(Y=1)=σ(a+blogit(p̂))P(Y=1)=\sigma(a+b\,\text{logit}(\hat p)). Two parameters, so it is stable on modest data, but it can only apply a smooth monotone correction.
  • Isotonic regression fits the best non-decreasing step function. It is far more flexible and can correct any monotone distortion, but it needs more data and can overfit into a staircase.

The recalibration map must be fitted on data not used to evaluate it, otherwise the improvement is an illusion. Here we split the 2023 test season in half by game: fit the maps on one half, evaluate on the other.

set.seed(431)
test_games <- unique(predictions$game_id)
fitting_games <- sample(test_games, floor(length(test_games) / 2))

fitting_half <- predictions %>% filter(game_id %in% fitting_games)
evaluation_half <- predictions %>% filter(!game_id %in% fitting_games)


logit <- function(p) {
  p <- pmin(pmax(p, 1e-6), 1 - 1e-6)
  log(p / (1 - p))
}

inverse_logit <- function(z) 1 / (1 + exp(-z))

area_under_curve <- function(truth, probability) {
  positive <- truth == 1
  ranks <- rank(probability, ties.method = "average")
  (sum(ranks[positive]) - sum(positive) * (sum(positive) + 1) / 2) /
    (sum(positive) * sum(!positive))
}

recalibrate <- function(label, fitting_probability, evaluation_probability) {
  platt <- glm(
    y ~ z,
    data = data.frame(y = fitting_half$home_win, z = logit(fitting_probability)),
    family = binomial()
  )
  platt_probability <- as.numeric(predict(
    platt,
    newdata = data.frame(z = logit(evaluation_probability)),
    type = "response"
  ))

  isotonic <- as.stepfun(isoreg(fitting_probability, fitting_half$home_win))
  isotonic_probability <- pmin(pmax(isotonic(evaluation_probability), 1e-6), 1 - 1e-6)

  truth <- evaluation_half$home_win
  tibble::tibble(
    forecast = label,
    map = c("none", "Platt", "isotonic"),
    brier = c(
      brier_score(truth, evaluation_probability),
      brier_score(truth, platt_probability),
      brier_score(truth, isotonic_probability)
    ),
    log_loss = c(
      log_loss(truth, evaluation_probability),
      log_loss(truth, platt_probability),
      log_loss(truth, isotonic_probability)
    ),
    auc = c(
      area_under_curve(truth, evaluation_probability),
      area_under_curve(truth, platt_probability),
      area_under_curve(truth, isotonic_probability)
    )
  )
}

sharpen <- function(p) inverse_logit(2.5 * logit(p))

bind_rows(
  recalibrate(
    "logistic (already calibrated)",
    fitting_half$logistic,
    evaluation_half$logistic
  ),
  recalibrate(
    "logistic, sharpened",
    sharpen(fitting_half$logistic),
    sharpen(evaluation_half$logistic)
  )
)

Recalibration repairs a broken model. Sharpening drove log loss from .532 to .603; Platt scaling brings it back to .549, recovering nearly all of the damage. The fitted slope is close to 1/2.51/2.5 of the calibrated slope — the map has effectively inverted the distortion.

Recalibration does not improve an already-calibrated model; it costs you. Applied to the untouched logistic forecast, Platt scaling makes log loss slightly worse, because it spends two parameters estimated from finite data to correct a problem that was not there. Isotonic is worse still, having spent far more flexibility on noise. Recalibrate when a calibration plot shows a problem, not by reflex.

Note

Isotonic regression depends only on the order of the fitting predictions, so its results are unchanged by any monotone distortion of the input. That is why the isotonic rows are identical for the original and sharpened forecasts.

The step-like path from a single CART model is a direct consequence of piecewise-constant leaves. Ensembles usually create finer probability changes, while logistic regression changes smoothly according to its linear log-odds equation.

10 Recommended final project workflow

  1. Define the prediction moment and the information available at that moment.
  2. Establish logistic regression as the baseline.
  3. Compare a pruned tree, random forest, and one boosted-tree implementation.
  4. Tune only on grouped development splits.
  5. Lock the modeling decisions and evaluate once on a future season.
  6. Report Brier score, log loss, calibration, and performance by game stage.
  7. Inspect several game trajectories and document failure cases.
  8. Recommend a model by balancing quality, interpretability, speed, and maintenance.

12 Key takeaways

  • A scoring rule is proper when honest reporting minimizes the expected score, and strictly proper when honesty is the only minimizer.
  • The Brier score decomposes as q(1−q)+(p−q)2q(1-q)+(p-q)^2 and log loss as H(q)+DKL(q∥p)H(q)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p); both are strictly proper.
  • Accuracy depends on the forecast only through which side of the threshold it falls on. It is not strictly proper at t=0.5t=0.5 and not proper at all for teq0.5t eq0.5.
  • Never select, tune, or rank a probability model on accuracy.
  • Calibration asks whether a stated 70% is really 70%. It is orthogonal to ranking: AUC cannot see a calibration failure at all.
  • Recalibration (Platt, isotonic) repairs a miscalibrated model but must be fitted on data not used to evaluate it, and it helps nothing when calibration is already sound.
  • Check calibration in the segment where the model will be used, not only season-wide.
  • Compare models on a future season, check calibration, and confirm that a gap is larger than its bootstrap uncertainty before declaring a winner.
  • A complex model must beat the baseline to justify itself. In this lecture it does not, and reporting that is the analysis.
