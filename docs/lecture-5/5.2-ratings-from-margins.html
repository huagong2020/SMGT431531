5.2 Ratings from Margins: Least Squares, Ridge, and a Gaussian Prior

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • build the ±1\pm1 design matrix for game outcomes and explain why it has one fewer than full rank;
  • fit least-squares (Massey) ratings with a home-field term;
  • add a Gaussian prior on the ratings and show that the posterior mode is ridge regression;
  • estimate the prior variance and the noise variance by marginal likelihood and interpret the shrinkage;
  • fit the same model with rstanarm and read posterior intervals and comparison probabilities; and
  • evaluate margin-based ratings on held-out games.

2 The design matrix

Each game gg contributes one row. Let xgj=+1x_{gj}=+1 if team jj is the home team, −1-1 if it is the away team, and 00 otherwise, and let hg=1h_g=1 unless the game was played at a neutral site. The margin model is

yg=αhg+∑jxgjθj+εg=αhg+θhome(g)−θaway(g)+εg,εg∼N(0,σ2). y_g=\alpha h_g+\sum_{j}x_{gj}\theta_j+\varepsilon_g =\alpha h_g+\theta_{\text{home}(g)}-\theta_{\text{away}(g)}+\varepsilon_g, \qquad \varepsilon_g\sim N(0,\sigma^2).

This is the adjusted plus-minus design of Lecture 4 with teams in place of players and exactly one team on each side.

X <- build_design_matrix(games_2023)

Every row sums to zero, so the columns are linearly dependent and X𝖳XX^{\mathsf T}X is singular. Adding a constant to every θj\theta_j leaves every θhome−θaway\theta_{\text{home}}-\theta_{\text{away}} unchanged. Only differences between ratings are identified. Two standard fixes are to drop one team’s column (a reference team) or to impose ∑jθj=0\sum_j\theta_j=0. The functions below fit with a reference team and then recenter, which is equivalent.

A rating is the team’s expected margin against an average opponent on a neutral field, in points. The home-advantage estimate of about 2.6 points is in line with the historical NFL value of roughly 2.5 to 3 points, although the edge has been smaller in several recent seasons.

3.1 How noisy are they?

With σ≈13\sigma\approx13 points per game and 17 games, the standard error of a team’s rating is roughly σ/17≈3\sigma/\sqrt{17}\approx3 points, before accounting for the uncertainty in the opponents’ ratings that propagates through the schedule. Differences of a few points between teams are within noise.

4 A Gaussian prior on the ratings

Least squares treats every rating as a free parameter. A better assumption is that ratings come from a population: most teams are near average and a few are far from it. Write

θj∼N(0,τ2), \theta_j\sim N(0,\tau^2),

which, as Lecture 4 showed, makes the posterior mode the ridge estimator

𝜽̂=(X𝖳X+λI)−1X𝖳(𝒚−α̂𝒉),λ=σ2τ2. \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=\left(X^{\mathsf T}X+\lambda I\right)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\left(\mathbf y-\hat\alpha\mathbf h\right), \qquad \lambda=\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}.

NoteMathematical details: why subtract estimated home advantage?

The model and independent Gaussian prior are

𝒚=α𝒉+X𝜽+𝜺,𝜺∼N(𝟎,σ2I),𝜽∼N(𝟎,τ2I). \mathbf y=\alpha\mathbf h+X\boldsymbol\theta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim N(\mathbf0,\sigma^2I), \qquad \boldsymbol\theta\sim N(\mathbf0,\tau^2I).

Here each margin is home minus away, so hg=1h_g=1 for a home venue and hg=0h_g=0 at a neutral site. With a flat prior on α\alpha and fixed positive variances, maximizing the posterior is equivalent to minimizing

Q(𝜽,α)=∥𝒚−α𝒉−X𝜽∥2+λ∥𝜽∥2,λ=σ2/τ2. Q(\boldsymbol\theta,\alpha) =\|\mathbf y-\alpha\mathbf h-X\boldsymbol\theta\|^2 +\lambda\|\boldsymbol\theta\|^2, \qquad \lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2.

Differentiating with respect to the ratings gives

−2X𝖳(𝒚−α𝒉−X𝜽)+2λ𝜽=𝟎. -2X^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y-\alpha\mathbf h-X\boldsymbol\theta) +2\lambda\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf0.

If α\alpha were known, the solution would use 𝒚−α𝒉\mathbf y-\alpha\mathbf h. At the joint optimum, its value is α̂\hat\alpha, giving

𝜽̂=(X𝖳X+λI)−1X𝖳(𝒚−α̂𝒉). \hat{\boldsymbol\theta} =(X^{\mathsf T}X+\lambda I)^{-1} X^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y-\hat\alpha\mathbf h).

The hat means that home advantage is estimated. It introduces no extra approximation into this equation. The two estimates satisfy simultaneous equations; for example, when 𝒉𝖳𝒉>0\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}\mathbf h>0,

α̂=𝒉𝖳(𝒚−X𝜽̂)𝒉𝖳𝒉. \hat\alpha=\frac{\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y-X\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})} {\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}\mathbf h}.

The penalty also solves the identification problem: pulling every rating toward zero pins down the constant that least squares could not.

NoteMathematical details: rating levels and the last team’s rating

Because X𝟏=𝟎X\mathbf1=\mathbf0, adding a constant to all ratings leaves predicted margins unchanged. If we impose the zero-sum convention, the last rating satisfies

θJ=−∑j=1J−1θj. \theta_J=-\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}\theta_j.

This is a constraint defining the baseline, not another observation. With the independent zero-centered Gaussian prior used above, the ridge mode automatically has zero sum: multiply its normal equations by 𝟏𝖳\mathbf1^{\mathsf T} to obtain λ𝟏𝖳𝜽̂=0\lambda\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=0. Individual prior or posterior draws need not sum to zero unless explicitly centered or constrained. An explicitly constrained Gaussian prior has dependent ratings, so it should not be confused with independent priors on all JJ ratings.

For a team with GG games against average opponents, the ridge estimate is approximately

θ̂j≈GG+λm‾j, \hat\theta_j\approx\frac{G}{G+\lambda}\,\bar m_j,

where m‾j\bar m_j is its adjusted average margin. With σ=13\sigma=13 and τ=5\tau=5, λ≈6.8\lambda\approx6.8, and a 17-game season keeps about 17/23.8≈71%17/23.8\approx71\% of the observed deviation.

NoteMathematical details: ridge shrinkage

4.1 Deriving the approximation for one team

For a team-specific view, orient each margin yjiy_{ji} as team jj’s score minus its opponent’s score. Define sji=1s_{ji}=1 when team jj is home, −1-1 when away, and 00 at a neutral venue. This differs from the home-oriented hgh_g above. Then

yji=θj−θk(i)+αsji+εji. y_{ji}=\theta_j-\theta_{k(i)}+\alpha s_{ji}+\varepsilon_{ji}.

Holding the opponent ratings and home advantage fixed, define

aji=yji+θk(i)−αsji. a_{ji}=y_{ji}+\theta_{k(i)}-\alpha s_{ji}.

Each ajia_{ji} measures θj\theta_j with Gaussian noise. Completing the square gives the conditional posterior

θj∣other ratings, data,α,σ2,τ2∼N(∑iajiG+λ,σ2G+λ). \theta_j\mid\text{other ratings, data},\alpha,\sigma^2,\tau^2 \sim N\!\left( \frac{\sum_i a_{ji}}{G+\lambda}, \frac{\sigma^2}{G+\lambda} \right).

At the joint ridge solution, the same coordinate equation uses estimated opponent ratings and α̂\hat\alpha.

A normal distribution’s mode equals its mean. At the joint posterior mode, each coordinate must maximize its conditional posterior, evaluated at the other fitted parameters. Hence θ̂j=∑i(yji+θ̂k(i)−α̂sji)G+λ. \hat\theta_j = \frac{ \sum_i\left(y_{ji}+\hat\theta_{k(i)}-\hat\alpha s_{ji}\right) }{G+\lambda}.

Define mi=yji−α̂sji,m‾j=1G∑imi,θ̂¯opp,j=1G∑iθ̂k(i). m_i=y_{ji}-\hat\alpha s_{ji}, \qquad \bar m_j=\frac1G\sum_i m_i, \qquad \overline{\hat\theta}_{\mathrm{opp},j} =\frac1G\sum_i\hat\theta_{k(i)}. Then the exact coordinate equation is θ̂j=GG+λ(m‾j+θ̂¯opp,j). \boxed{ \hat\theta_j =\frac{G}{G+\lambda} \left(\bar m_j+\overline{\hat\theta}_{\mathrm{opp},j}\right). }

If the fitted opponent ratings average approximately zero, θ̂¯opp,j≈0, \overline{\hat\theta}_{\mathrm{opp},j}\approx0,

then

θ̂j≈GG+λm‾j. \boxed{ \hat\theta_j\approx\frac{G}{G+\lambda}\bar m_j. }

4.2 What does retaining 71% of the deviation mean?

With σ=13\sigma=13 and τ=5\tau=5, λ=169/25=6.76\lambda=169/25=6.76. Using the rounded λ≈6.8\lambda\approx6.8 gives

1717+6.8≈0.714. \frac{17}{17+6.8}\approx0.714.

Thus an adjusted average margin of +10+10 becomes a rating of about +7.1+7.1, and −10-10 becomes about −7.1-7.1. The estimate retains about 71% of the observed distance from zero and shrinks the remaining 29% toward the average-team rating. This is neither a standard deviation nor a probability of winning. More games reduce shrinkage; larger game noise or a tighter prior increase it.

4.3 Estimating the variances

Rather than choose λ\lambda by hand, estimate τ2\tau^2 and σ2\sigma^2 by maximizing the marginal likelihood of the margins after integrating the ratings out,

𝒚−α𝒉∼N(𝟎,τ2XX𝖳+σ2I). \mathbf y-\alpha\mathbf h\sim N\!\left(\mathbf 0,\ \tau^2XX^{\mathsf T}+\sigma^2I\right).

This is what a multilevel model does.

NoteMathematical details: integrating out the ratings

Set 𝒛=𝒚−α𝒉\mathbf z=\mathbf y-\alpha\mathbf h. The marginal density averages the conditional density over all possible ratings:

p(𝒛∣τ2,σ2)=∫ℝJp(𝒛∣𝜽,σ2)p(𝜽∣τ2)d𝜽. p(\mathbf z\mid\tau^2,\sigma^2) =\int_{\mathbb R^J} p(\mathbf z\mid\boldsymbol\theta,\sigma^2) p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\tau^2)\,d\boldsymbol\theta.

This is the law of total probability. Generating 𝒛\mathbf z by first drawing ratings and then drawing game noise is equivalent to forming 𝒛=X𝜽+𝜺\mathbf z=X\boldsymbol\theta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon. Linear transformations and sums of independent Gaussian variables are Gaussian, so

E(𝒛)=𝟎,Var⁡(𝒛)=τ2XX𝖳+σ2I=:V,𝒛∼N(𝟎,V). E(\mathbf z)=\mathbf0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf z)=\tau^2XX^{\mathsf T}+\sigma^2I=:V, \qquad \boxed{\mathbf z\sim N(\mathbf0,V).}

This evaluates the integral using a Gaussian identity. Knowing the mean and covariance is sufficient because we have already established that the distribution is Gaussian. For a scalar example, θ∼N(0,4)\theta\sim N(0,4) and z∣θ∼N(θ,9)z\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,9) imply z∼N(0,13)z\sim N(0,13): the two independent sources of variance add. Integrating out ratings retains their uncertainty; it does not set them to zero.

For an explicit calculation, let nn be the number of games and define

A=X𝖳Xσ2+Iτ2,𝒃=X𝖳𝒛σ2. A=\frac{X^{\mathsf T}X}{\sigma^2}+\frac{I}{\tau^2}, \qquad \mathbf b=\frac{X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf z}{\sigma^2}.

The exponent in the product of the two Gaussian densities can be written as

∥𝒛−X𝜽∥2σ2+∥𝜽∥2τ2=(𝜽−A−1𝒃)𝖳A(𝜽−A−1𝒃)+𝒛𝖳𝒛σ2−𝒃𝖳A−1𝒃. \frac{\|\mathbf z-X\boldsymbol\theta\|^2}{\sigma^2} +\frac{\|\boldsymbol\theta\|^2}{\tau^2} =(\boldsymbol\theta-A^{-1}\mathbf b)^{\mathsf T} A(\boldsymbol\theta-A^{-1}\mathbf b) +\frac{\mathbf z^{\mathsf T}\mathbf z}{\sigma^2} -\mathbf b^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}\mathbf b.

Use the Gaussian integral

∫ℝJexp⁡[−12(𝜽−A−1𝒃)𝖳A(𝜽−A−1𝒃)]d𝜽=(2π)J/2|A|−1/2. \int_{\mathbb R^J} \exp\!\left[-\tfrac12(\boldsymbol\theta-A^{-1}\mathbf b)^{\mathsf T} A(\boldsymbol\theta-A^{-1}\mathbf b)\right]d\boldsymbol\theta =(2\pi)^{J/2}|A|^{-1/2}.

Including the original normalizing constants, the marginal density becomes

(2π)−n/2(σ2)−n/2(τ2)−J/2|A|−1/2exp⁡[−12(𝒛𝖳𝒛σ2−𝒃𝖳A−1𝒃)]. (2\pi)^{-n/2}(\sigma^2)^{-n/2}(\tau^2)^{-J/2}|A|^{-1/2} \exp\!\left[-\tfrac12\left( \frac{\mathbf z^{\mathsf T}\mathbf z}{\sigma^2} -\mathbf b^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}\mathbf b\right)\right].

This is the N(𝟎,V)N(\mathbf0,V) density. Consequently the marginal log likelihood is

ℓ(α,τ2,σ2)=−12[nlog(2π)+log|V|+(𝒚−α𝒉)𝖳V−1(𝒚−α𝒉)]. \ell(\alpha,\tau^2,\sigma^2) =-\tfrac12\left[n\log(2\pi)+\log|V| +(\mathbf y-\alpha\mathbf h)^{\mathsf T}V^{-1}(\mathbf y-\alpha\mathbf h)\right].

Maximizing it estimates home advantage and both variances, giving λ̂=σ̂2/τ̂2\hat\lambda=\hat\sigma^2/\hat\tau^2.

The calculation below uses the matrix determinant lemma and the Woodbury identity so that only 32×3232\times32 matrices are inverted.

home <- 1 - games_2023$neutral_site
XtX <- crossprod(X)
J <- ncol(X)
G <- nrow(X)

marginal_log_likelihood <- function(parameters) {
  alpha <- parameters[[1]]
  tau2 <- exp(2 * parameters[[2]])
  sigma2 <- exp(2 * parameters[[3]])
  r <- games_2023$margin - alpha * home
  Xtr <- crossprod(X, r)
  inner <- XtX + (sigma2 / tau2) * diag(J)
  log_det <- (G - J) * log(sigma2) + as.numeric(determinant(sigma2 * diag(J) + tau2 * XtX)$modulus)
  quadratic <- (sum(r^2) - as.numeric(t(Xtr) %*% solve(inner, Xtr))) / sigma2
  -0.5 * (log_det + quadratic + G * log(2 * pi))
}

ml_fit <- optim(c(2, log(5), log(13)), function(p) -marginal_log_likelihood(p), method = "BFGS")

alpha_hat <- ml_fit$par[[1]]
tau_hat <- exp(ml_fit$par[[2]])
sigma_hat <- exp(ml_fit$par[[3]])
lambda_hat <- sigma_hat^2 / tau_hat^2

data.frame(
  home_advantage = alpha_hat,
  tau = tau_hat,
  sigma = sigma_hat,
  lambda = lambda_hat,
  retained_share_17_games = 17 / (17 + lambda_hat)
) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 2)
home_advantage tau sigma lambda retained_share_17_games

5 Bayesian regression with an estimated prior scale

rstanarm::stan_glm() samples coefficients and residual scale with the team prior scale fixed at the empirical estimate τ̂\hat\tau. This is a Bayesian regression conditional on a data-estimated prior scale, rather than a fully Bayesian hierarchy for team strength.

6 Held-out evaluation

Fit on weeks 1 through 14 and predict weeks 15 through 18. Turn each predicted margin into a home-win probability with the fitted noise scale,

P(home wins)=Φ(α̂hg+θ̂home−θ̂awayσ̂). P(\text{home wins})=\Phi\!\left(\frac{\hat\alpha h_g+\hat\theta_{\text{home}}-\hat\theta_{\text{away}}}{\hat\sigma}\right).

NoteWhy divide by the fitted noise scale?

Dividing by σ̂\hat\sigma expresses the predicted margin in standard deviations, because Φ\Phi is the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal random variable.

Write the predicted home-team margin as μ̂g=α̂hg+θ̂home−θ̂away\hat\mu_g=\hat\alpha h_g+\hat\theta_{\text{home}}-\hat\theta_{\text{away}}. Under the fitted model,

Mg=μ̂g+εg,εg∼N(0,σ̂2). M_g=\hat\mu_g+\varepsilon_g,\qquad \varepsilon_g\sim N(0,\hat\sigma^2).

The home team wins when Mg>0M_g>0. Standardizing the noise gives

P(Mg>0)=P(εgσ̂>−μ̂gσ̂)=1−Φ(−μ̂gσ̂)=Φ(μ̂gσ̂), P(M_g>0) =P\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_g}{\hat\sigma}>-\frac{\hat\mu_g}{\hat\sigma}\right) =1-\Phi\!\left(-\frac{\hat\mu_g}{\hat\sigma}\right) =\Phi\!\left(\frac{\hat\mu_g}{\hat\sigma}\right),

where the last equality uses the symmetry of the standard normal distribution. Here σ̂\hat\sigma estimates the game-to-game noise standard deviation around the expected margin.

For example, a predicted 7-point advantage with σ̂=14\hat\sigma=14 is a 7/14=0.57/14=0.5-standard-deviation advantage, giving Φ(0.5)≈0.69\Phi(0.5)\approx0.69, or about a 69% chance of winning. More game-to-game noise means the same predicted advantage gives less certainty of a win.

train <- games_2023 %>% filter(week <= 14)
test <- games_2023 %>% filter(week >= 15, tie == 0)

# Reestimate every tuning and noise parameter using training games only.
train_hyper <- fit_margin_hyperparameters(train)
sigma_train <- train_hyper$sigma
lambda_train <- train_hyper$lambda

predict_margin <- function(ratings, home_advantage, games) {
  home_advantage * (1 - games$neutral_site) + ratings[games$home_team] - ratings[games$away_team]
}

evaluate <- function(ratings, home_advantage, label) {
  predicted <- predict_margin(ratings, home_advantage, test)
  probability <- pnorm(predicted / sigma_train)
  data.frame(
    model = label,
    rmse_margin = sqrt(mean((test$margin - predicted)^2)),
    log_loss = log_loss(test$home_win, probability),
    brier = brier_score(test$home_win, probability)
  )
}

massey_train <- massey_ratings(train)
ridge_train <- ridge_ratings(train, lambda_train)
ridge_strong <- ridge_ratings(train, 4 * lambda_train)

bind_rows(
  evaluate(setNames(rep(0, J), colnames(X)), train_hyper$home_advantage, "home advantage only"),
  evaluate(massey_train$ratings, massey_train$home_advantage, "least squares"),
  evaluate(ridge_train$ratings, ridge_train$home_advantage, "ridge (marginal likelihood)"),
  evaluate(ridge_strong$ratings, ridge_strong$home_advantage, "ridge (four times the penalty)")
) %>%

Sixty-odd test games cannot separate the models sharply, and the ordering can change with the split. The consistent lesson across seasons is that shrinkage helps early in a season, when least squares is badly overfit, and matters less once every team has played a dozen games.

8 Key takeaways

  • Game outcomes fit a regression with one ±1\pm1 column per team and a home-field term; least squares gives schedule-adjusted ratings.
  • The design has one fewer than full rank; a constraint or a prior identifies the level.
  • A Gaussian prior on ratings is ridge regression with λ=σ2/τ2\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2, and marginal likelihood estimates both variances.
  • NFL ratings have posterior standard deviations near three points after one season; treat small differences as noise.
  • Posterior draws turn the ratings into probabilities of comparisons.
