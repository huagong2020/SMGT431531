5.1 Team Evaluation: Outcomes, Luck, and Schedules

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • define team strength as a latent quantity and list the observable outcomes that inform it;
  • compare win percentage with point differential as measures of strength and explain the Pythagorean expectation;
  • decompose the variance of win percentage into talent and luck and estimate the number of games at which they are equal;
  • show why an unadjusted margin depends on schedule and why adjustment requires solving for every team at once; and
  • describe the special features of soccer outcomes: low scores, draws, and home advantage.

2 Strength is latent; outcomes are observed

Let θj\theta_j denote the strength of team jj: the expected margin against an average opponent on a neutral field. No game reveals θj\theta_j directly. A game between teams ii and jj reveals a noisy function of θi−θj\theta_i-\theta_j: a winner, a margin, or a pair of goal counts. Every rating system in this lecture is a rule for turning those outcomes into estimates of θ\theta, and every one of them has to deal with three facts.

  1. Outcomes are noisy. A single NFL game has a margin standard deviation near 14 points around its expectation.
  2. Schedules differ. A team’s results depend on who it played.
  3. Home matters. The home team wins more often than not in every major league.

Win percentage throws away the size of every victory. Point differential keeps it. Which is the better measure of strength? One test is prediction: which one better forecasts the next season’s record?

standings <- team_season_table(nfl)

next_season <- standings %>%
  select(season, team, win_pct, point_diff) %>%
  inner_join(
    standings %>% transmute(season = season - 1, team, next_win_pct = win_pct),
    by = c("season", "team")
  )

next_season %>%
  summarize(
    seasons = paste(unique(season), collapse = ", "),
    cor_win_pct_with_next = cor(win_pct, next_win_pct),

  ) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)

2022, 2023 0.35 0.323

With only two season-to-season transitions the two correlations are statistically indistinguishable. Over long histories point differential has been the better predictor, which is the basis of the Pythagorean expectation below: a 30-point win and a 1-point win are different evidence about strength, and the record discards the difference.

3.1 Pythagorean expectation

Bill James observed that a team’s win percentage is close to

WP̂=PFkPFk+PAk, \widehat{\mathrm{WP}}=\frac{\mathrm{PF}^{\,k}}{\mathrm{PF}^{\,k}+\mathrm{PA}^{\,k}},

where PF\mathrm{PF} and PA\mathrm{PA} are points for and against. The exponent kk depends on the sport; for the NFL a common value is 2.37. The formula converts a point differential into an expected record, and the gap between actual and expected wins is a natural definition of luck.

standings_2023 <- standings_2023 %>%
  mutate(
    pythagorean = pythagorean(points_for, points_against, exponent = 2.37),
    luck_wins = (win_pct - pythagorean) * games
  )

standings_2023 %>%
  arrange(desc(luck_wins)) %>%
  select(team, win_pct, pythagorean, luck_wins) %>%
  slice(c(1:4, 29:32)) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)

4 Talent and luck

Treat each team’s season win percentage as a noisy measurement of its true winning probability pjp_j:

WP̂j=pj+ej,E[ej∣pj]=0,Var⁡(ej∣pj)=pj(1−pj)G, \widehat{\mathrm{WP}}_j=p_j+e_j, \qquad E[e_j\mid p_j]=0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(e_j\mid p_j)=\frac{p_j(1-p_j)}{G},

where GG is the number of games. This assumes independent, tie-free games with a constant win probability for each team. Across the league, the law of total variance gives

Var⁡(WP̂)=Var⁡(p)⏟talent+E[p(1−p)]G⏟luck. \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\widehat{\mathrm{WP}}\right) =\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(p)}_{\text{talent}} +\underbrace{\frac{E[p(1-p)]}{G}}_{\text{luck}}.

The exact luck variance under this model is obtained by averaging the conditional variance across teams. Since E[e∣p]=0E[e\mid p]=0 and E[p2]=Var⁡(p)+p‾2E[p^2]=\operatorname{Var}(p)+\bar p^2, where p‾=E[p]\bar p=E[p],

Var⁡(e)=E[Var⁡(e∣p)]+Var⁡(E[e∣p])=E[p]−E[p2]G=p‾(1−p‾)−Var⁡(p)G. \operatorname{Var}(e) =E[\operatorname{Var}(e\mid p)]+\operatorname{Var}(E[e\mid p]) =\frac{E[p]-E[p^2]}{G} =\frac{\bar p(1-\bar p)-\operatorname{Var}(p)}{G}.

For a simple approximation, neglect the Var⁡(p)\operatorname{Var}(p) term in this numerator. With p‾=0.5\bar p=0.5, luck variance is then approximately 0.25/G0.25/G. This is the signal-plus-noise decomposition of Lecture 4 applied to teams: subtract the approximate luck variance from the observed variance to estimate talent variance. The calculation below treats season length as 17 games; actual ties, unequal schedules, and shared games make this an illustrative decomposition.

luck_variance <- 0.25 / 17  # Approximation, not the exact binomial mixture variance.
observed_variance <- var(standings$win_pct)
talent_variance <- observed_variance - luck_variance

data.frame(
  observed_variance = observed_variance,
  approximate_luck_variance = luck_variance,
  estimated_talent_variance = talent_variance,
  talent_share = talent_variance / observed_variance,
  games_for_equal_share = 0.25 / talent_variance
) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 4)
observed_variance approximate_luck_variance estimated_talent_variance talent_share games_for_equal_share
0.0351 0.0147 0.0204 0.5806 12.2779

Roughly 40% of the variance in NFL records over these three seasons is attributed to luck by this approximation, and talent and luck contribute equally at about twelve games, so a 17-game season is only modestly more signal than noise. The same reasoning gives the approximate linear shrinkage estimate of a team’s true winning probability,

p̂j=p‾+Var⁡(p)Var⁡(p)+p‾(1−p‾)/G(WP̂j−p‾), \hat p_j=\bar p+\frac{\operatorname{Var}(p)}{\operatorname{Var}(p)+\bar p(1-\bar p)/G}\left(\widehat{\mathrm{WP}}_j-\bar p\right),

which regresses every record toward .500 by a factor equal to the approximate talent share. Here the variance components are replaced by their estimates above.

5 Schedules

A team’s average margin also depends on whom it played. Define the naive rating as the average margin,

rj(0)=1Gj∑g∈𝒢j(points for−points against)g. r_j^{(0)}=\frac{1}{G_j}\sum_{g\in\mathcal G_j}\left(\text{points for}-\text{points against}\right)_g.

A fair rating should add back the quality of the opposition: a team that averages +3+3 against opponents who are themselves +2+2 is better than a team that averages +3+3 against opponents who are −2-2. That suggests the iteration

rj(t+1)=1Gj∑g∈𝒢j[marging+ropp(g)(t)], r_j^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{G_j}\sum_{g\in\mathcal G_j}\left[\text{margin}_g+r^{(t)}_{\text{opp}(g)}\right],

which repeatedly adjusts each rating by the average current rating of its opponents.

games_2023 <- nfl %>% filter(season == 2023)
teams <- sort(unique(games_2023$home_team))

long <- bind_rows(
  games_2023 %>% transmute(team = home_team, opponent = away_team, margin = margin),
  games_2023 %>% transmute(team = away_team, opponent = home_team, margin = -margin)
)

rating <- setNames(rep(0, length(teams)), teams)
for (iteration in seq_len(50)) {
  rating <- long %>%
    mutate(opponent_rating = rating[opponent]) %>%
    group_by(team) %>%
    summarize(rating = mean(margin + opponent_rating), .groups = "drop") %>%
    with(setNames(rating, team))
  rating <- rating - mean(rating)
}

schedule_adjusted <- long %>%
  group_by(team) %>%
  summarize(average_margin = mean(margin), .groups = "drop") %>%
  mutate(
    adjusted_rating = rating[team],
    strength_of_schedule = adjusted_rating - average_margin
  ) %>%
  arrange(desc(adjusted_rating))

schedule_adjusted %>%
  slice_head(n = 8) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 2)
team average_margin adjusted_rating strength_of_schedule

The iteration converges to a fixed point in which every rating equals average margin plus average opponent rating. Tutorial 5.2 shows that this fixed point is the least-squares solution of a regression with one indicator column per team, which is a far more useful way to think about it because it extends to priors, uncertainty, and different outcomes.

6 Soccer outcomes

Soccer changes the problem in three ways.

epl %>%
  group_by(season) %>%
  summarize(
    matches = n(),
    home_goals = mean(home_goals),
    away_goals = mean(away_goals),
    home_win = mean(result == "H"),
    draw = mean(result == "D"),
    away_win = mean(result == "A"),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) %>%
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)
season matches home_goals away_goals home_win draw away_win
2021-22 380 1.513 1.305 0.429 0.232 0.339
2022-23 380 1.634 1.218 0.484 0.229 0.287
2023-24 380 1.800 1.479 0.461 0.216 0.324
  • Low scores. With about 1.5 home goals and 1.2 away goals per match, the margin takes only a handful of values, and a count model is more natural than a normal one.
  • Draws. About a quarter of matches are drawn. A win-loss model must handle a third outcome.
  • Home advantage. The home team scores about 0.3 goals more and wins about 45% of matches, which is a large effect in a low-scoring sport.
goal_counts <- c(epl$home_goals, epl$away_goals)
goal_table <- data.frame(goals = 0:7) %>%
  mutate(
    observed = sapply(goals, function(g) mean(goal_counts == g)),
    poisson = dpois(goals, mean(goal_counts))
  )

ggplot(goal_table, aes(goals)) +
  geom_col(aes(y = observed), fill = "#03a9e6", alpha = 0.7) +
  geom_point(aes(y = poisson), color = "black", size = 2) +
  labs(x = "Goals scored by one team", y = "Proportion of team-matches", caption = "Points: Poisson with the same mean") +
  theme_minimal()

The Poisson distribution fits goal counts well, which is the foundation of the model in Tutorial 5.4.

7 Practice

  1. A team goes 12-5 with a point differential of +20+20. Explain in one sentence why its Pythagorean record is far below 12-5.
  2. Using the talent variance estimated above, what is the shrinkage estimate of true winning probability for a 13-4 team?
  3. Why does the schedule-adjustment iteration need to recenter the ratings at every step?
  4. Give one reason a normal model for margins is less appropriate for soccer than for the NFL.
TipShow answers
  1. A +20+20 differential over 17 games is about +1.2+1.2 per game, which corresponds to only slightly better than a .500 team; the 12-5 record came from winning close games.
  2. p̂=0.5+s(13/17−0.5)\hat p=0.5+s(13/17-0.5) where ss is the approximate talent share; with s≈0.58s\approx0.58 the estimate is about .65.
  3. Adding the average opponent rating to every team’s rating shifts the whole set by a constant, so without recentering the ratings would drift; only differences between ratings are identified.
  4. Margins in soccer are small integers with many zeros, so a continuous symmetric distribution cannot represent draws or the discreteness.

8 Key takeaways

  • Team strength is latent; outcomes are noisy functions of strength differences plus home advantage.
  • Point differential carries more information than the record and predicts future records at least as well.
  • Roughly 40% of the variance in NFL records is attributed to luck by the simple approximation; shrink records toward .500 accordingly.
  • Schedule adjustment is a system of equations, one per team, which is a regression in disguise.
  • Soccer needs count models and a third outcome.
