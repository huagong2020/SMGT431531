5.4 Goals as Counts: A Bayesian Multilevel Poisson Model

Author

Hua Gong

1 Learning objectives

After this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • write a Poisson regression for goals with crossed attack and defense effects;
  • explain the priors of a Bayesian multilevel Poisson model and read its posterior summaries;
  • carry out posterior predictive checks and say what each one tests;
  • convert posterior draws into match-outcome probabilities and compare them with bookmaker odds;
  • replay a season from the posterior to attach probabilities to league positions; and
  • describe the model’s main limitations and the standard extensions.

2 The model

Each match produces two counts. Let YmHY_{m}^{H} and YmAY_{m}^{A} be the home and away goals in match mm between home team ii and away team jj. The Poisson model (Maher, 1982) is

YmH∼Poisson(λmH),log⁡λmH=μ+β+ai+dj, Y_m^{H}\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_m^{H}), \qquad \log\lambda_m^{H}=\mu+\beta+a_i+d_j,

YmA∼Poisson(λmA),log⁡λmA=μ+aj+di, Y_m^{A}\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_m^{A}), \qquad \log\lambda_m^{A}=\mu+a_j+d_i,

where μ\mu is the log of the baseline scoring rate, β\beta is the home advantage on the log scale, aa is a team’s attack effect, and dd is the effect of facing that team’s defense. A positive djd_j means opponents score more against team jj; a good defense has a negative dd. Home and away goals are treated as independent given the rates.

On the count scale, eβe^{\beta} multiplies the home team’s expected goals and eaie^{a_i} multiplies goals for team ii’s attack. The log link turns the additive model into a multiplicative one, which is what keeps expected goals positive.

2.1 The multilevel layer

The attack and defense effects are crossed random effects, exactly as in Lecture 4:

ai∼N(0,τa2),dj∼N(0,τd2). a_i\sim N(0,\tau_a^2), \qquad d_j\sim N(0,\tau_d^2).

With only 38 matches per team, shrinkage matters. A team that scored freely in its first ten matches is pulled toward the league until the evidence accumulates.

3 The Bayesian fit

The model was fitted with rstanarm::stan_glmer() and stored in data/team_results.rds. The call and the priors were:

library(rstanarm)

poisson_fit <- stan_glmer(
  goals ~ home + (1 | attack) + (1 | defense),
  data = goals_long,
  family = poisson(),
  prior_intercept = normal(0, 1),
  prior = normal(0, 1),
  prior_covariance = decov(scale = 0.5),
  chains = 4,
  iter = 2000,
  seed = 431
)

rstanarm applies prior_intercept after centering fixed predictors. With h‾=0.5\bar h=0.5, the centered intercept is αc=μ+h‾β\alpha_c=\mu+\bar h\beta. The specified priors are αc∼N(0,1)\alpha_c\sim N(0,1) and β∼N(0,1)\beta\sim N(0,1) independently, so the prior on the raw away baseline μ=αc−h‾β\mu=\alpha_c-\bar h\beta is induced rather than independent. For these one-dimensional random-effect blocks, decov(scale = 0.5) gives each standard deviation a gamma prior with shape 1 and scale 0.5. Check poisson$prior_summary for the fitted parameterization.

5 Posterior predictive checks

A Bayesian model can generate data. If simulated seasons look unlike the real one in some respect, the model is missing something. The stored replications object holds 500 simulated seasons, one per posterior draw, each with the same fixtures.

replications <- poisson$replications
dim(replications)
[1] 500 760

5.1 Goal counts

goal_check <- lapply(0:7, function(g) {
  replicated <- rowMeans(replications == g)

    goals = g,
    observed = mean(goals_long$goals == g),

5.2 Draws, high scores, and dispersion

Three summary statistics test different aspects of the model: the share of drawn matches tests the independence assumption at low scores, the maximum goals scored by one team in one match tests the tail, and the variance-to-mean ratio tests for overdispersion.

home_rows <- which(goals_long$home == 1)
away_candidates <- which(goals_long$home == 0)
away_rows <- away_candidates[match(goals_long$match_number[home_rows], goals_long$match_number[away_candidates])]
stopifnot(!anyNA(away_rows))

replicated_statistics <- data.frame(
  draw_share = rowMeans(replications[, home_rows] == replications[, away_rows]),
  max_goals = apply(replications, 1, max),
  dispersion = apply(replications, 1, var) / rowMeans(replications)
)

observed_statistics <- data.frame(
  draw_share = mean(goals_long$goals[home_rows] == goals_long$goals[away_rows]),
  max_goals = max(goals_long$goals),
  dispersion = var(goals_long$goals) / mean(goals_long$goals)
)

data.frame(
  statistic = names(observed_statistics),
  observed = as.numeric(observed_statistics),
  predictive_mean = colMeans(replicated_statistics),
  predictive_lower = apply(replicated_statistics, 2, quantile, 0.05),
  predictive_upper = apply(replicated_statistics, 2, quantile, 0.95),
  posterior_p_value = sapply(names(observed_statistics), function(s) mean(replicated_statistics[[s]] >= observed_statistics[[s]]))
) %>%

A posterior predictive pp-value near 0 or 1 flags a statistic the model cannot reproduce. In this season the draw share and the maximum score are reproduced comfortably. The dispersion statistic sits at the low end of its predictive distribution: the real goal counts are slightly less variable than the model’s simulated seasons, which mix over uncertain team effects. Across many seasons the independent-Poisson model tends to underpredict draws, which is the motivation for the Dixon-Coles correction described below.

6 Match probabilities

For a fixture between home team ii and away team jj, each posterior draw gives a pair of rates (λH,λA)(\lambda^H,\lambda^A). Under independence, the probability of the score (h,a)(h,a) is the product of two Poisson probabilities, and the outcome probabilities are sums over the score grid:

P(home win)=∑h>ae−λH(λH)hh!⋅e−λA(λA)aa!, P(\text{home win})=\sum_{h>a}\frac{e^{-\lambda^H}(\lambda^H)^h}{h!}\cdot\frac{e^{-\lambda^A}(\lambda^A)^a}{a!},

with the draw and away-win probabilities defined by h=ah=a and h<ah<a. Averaging over posterior draws integrates out the uncertainty in the rates.

set.seed(431)
draw_index <- sample(nrow(poisson$attack_draws), 200)

match_probabilities <- t(sapply(seq_len(nrow(epl_2324)), function(m) {
  h <- epl_2324$home_team[[m]]
  a <- epl_2324$away_team[[m]]
  lambda_home <- exp(poisson$intercept_draws[draw_index] + poisson$home_draws[draw_index] +
    poisson$attack_draws[draw_index, h] + poisson$defense_draws[draw_index, a])
  lambda_away <- exp(poisson$intercept_draws[draw_index] +
    poisson$attack_draws[draw_index, a] + poisson$defense_draws[draw_index, h])
  rowMeans(mapply(poisson_match_probabilities, lambda_home, lambda_away))
}))

7 Replaying the season

The replicated seasons also give a probability distribution over the league table. For each replication, compute every team’s points and rank.

home_goals_rep <- replications[, home_rows]
away_goals_rep <- replications[, away_rows]
home_teams <- goals_long$attack[home_rows]
away_teams <- goals_long$attack[away_rows]
teams <- colnames(poisson$attack_draws)

points_matrix <- t(sapply(seq_len(nrow(replications)), function(r) {
  home_points <- ifelse(home_goals_rep[r, ] > away_goals_rep[r, ], 3, ifelse(home_goals_rep[r, ] == away_goals_rep[r, ], 1, 0))
  away_points <- ifelse(away_goals_rep[r, ] > home_goals_rep[r, ], 3, ifelse(home_goals_rep[r, ] == away_goals_rep[r, ], 1, 0))
  tapply(c(home_points, away_points), c(home_teams, away_teams), sum)[teams]
}))

ranks <- t(apply(-points_matrix, 1, rank, ties.method = "random"))

actual_points <- tapply(
  c(ifelse(epl_2324$home_goals > epl_2324$away_goals, 3, ifelse(epl_2324$home_goals == epl_2324$away_goals, 1, 0)),

8 Limitations and extensions

  • Independence. Home and away goals are modeled as independent. Empirically there is mild dependence at low scores; Dixon and Coles (1997) multiply the probability of the four lowest scorelines (0-0, 1-0, 0-1, 1-1) by a correction with one parameter ρ\rho, which raises the draw probability. Bivariate Poisson models add a shared component instead.
  • Overdispersion. Goal counts are close to Poisson, so a negative binomial is rarely needed for team totals. Corners, shots, or fouls are typically overdispersed.
  • Time. Strength changes within a season through injuries, transfers, and form. Dixon and Coles weight past matches by e−ξte^{-\xi t}; Tutorial 5.5 lets strength evolve explicitly.
  • Expected goals. Replacing goals with expected goals as the response reduces noise but changes the target from what happened to what the chances were worth.

9 Practice

  1. A team has attack effect 0.25 and faces a defense with effect −0.15-0.15 at home, with μ=0.2\mu=0.2 and β=0.2\beta=0.2. What is its expected goal count?
  2. Why is the defense effect written with a positive sign in the linear predictor?
  3. Name one posterior predictive check that would detect a failure of the independence assumption and one that would not.
  4. Why should match probabilities be averaged over posterior draws rather than computed from the posterior means of the rates?
TipShow answers
  1. exp⁡(0.2+0.2+0.25−0.15)=e0.5≈1.65\exp(0.2+0.2+0.25-0.15)=e^{0.5}\approx1.65 goals.
  2. It is the effect of facing that defense on the opponent’s scoring rate; a good defense has a negative value. The sign is a convention that matches the (1 | defense) term.
  3. The draw share can detect it; the distribution of goals per team cannot, because independence concerns the joint distribution of the two counts.
  4. The outcome probabilities are nonlinear in the rates, so the average of the probabilities is not the probability at the average rates; averaging over draws also carries the uncertainty in the effects into the forecast.

10 Key takeaways

  • Soccer goals fit a Poisson regression with a log link, a home effect, and crossed attack and defense effects.
  • The multilevel priors shrink team effects, which matters over a 38-match season.
  • Posterior predictive checks compare simulated seasons with the real one and locate where the model falls short.
  • Outcome probabilities come from summing a score grid and averaging over posterior draws.
  • Replaying the season converts strength estimates into probabilities for league positions.
